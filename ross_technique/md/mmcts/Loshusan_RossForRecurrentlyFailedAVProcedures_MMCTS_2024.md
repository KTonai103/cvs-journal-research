The Ross procedure provides young patients with unrepairable aortic valve disease with a living pulmonary autograft that confers significant survival benefit and improved quality of life. However, the procedure is complicated, and surgeons can be reluctant to offer it as a solution, especially in complex re-operative scenarios. We present a young patient with symptomatic, severe aortic insufficiency who had undergone two failed aortic valve procedures for congenital bicuspid aortic valve disease within the prior year. They presented with recurrent congestive heart failure, patient prosthesis mismatch and a severe paravalvular leak. We performed a Ross procedure to restore aortic valve function and quality of life. Despite the increased re-operative complexity, these young patients have the most to benefit from pulmonary autograft reconstruction.

Many valve substitute options exist for aortic valve replacement (AVR) and include bioprostheses, mechanical valves, xenografts, homografts, pulmonary autografts and transcatheter aortic valve (AV) implants. In young patients, mounting evidence supports favourable long-term clinical outcomes following a Ross procedure over all other valve replacement options. Numerous studies have demonstrated the enduring survival benefits and enhanced quality of life associated with the Ross procedure \[1\]. Conversely, bioprosthetic AVR is linked to excess valve-related deaths, particularly in younger populations, with notable rates of structural valve deterioration and re-intervention \[2, 3\]. The survival superiority of the Ross operation has been consistently demonstrated across various single and multicentre cohort series, meta-analyses and a randomized control trial \[4\]. However, despite its demonstrated efficacy, the Ross procedure remains classified as a class IIb recommendation in the United States guidelines and is not even endorsed in European guidelines. In this case report, we demonstrate the procedural steps involved in treating a young patient who presented with heart failure, patient prosthesis mismatch and a severe paravalvular leak (PVL) and who underwent a third sternotomy and a Ross procedure for a failed bioprosthetic AV.

- 1 - Patient Presentation (0:13)
	The patient is a 27-year-old male with a history of bicuspid AV who underwent two AV operations within the past year. He had first undergone an AV repair that was complicated by recurrent severe aortic insufﬁciency that prompted an urgent re-operation three months later with a bioprosthetic AVR. He then presented a month later with recurrent congestive heart failure and New York Heart Association functional class III dyspnoea and was found to have patient prosthesis mismatch and a severe PVL. He sought widely for options for re-operation and decided to pursue a Ross procedure. His preoperative transthoracic echocardiographic scan demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55–60% with end systolic and end diastolic dimensions of 38 and 55 mm, respectively. The 21-mm Edwards Inspiris valve (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) had a mean/peak gradient of 38/62 mmHg, respectively, with moderate to severe aortic insufﬁciency from a PVL (Figure 1a). The patient travelled to our centre for a third operation within 12 months.
	![](https://mmcts.org/media/8141f8a8-8903-4522-86da-c60e5860a6c7) **Figure 1:** **(a)** Preoperative transthoracic echocardiographic short axis with a severe paravalvular leak; **(b)** arrow designating intraoperative anterior location of the paravalvular leak; **(c)** explantation of the prosthetic aortic valve; (d) harvesting the pulmonary autograft.
- 2 - Redo sternotomy and initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass (1:18)
	The patient underwent routine general anaesthesia with standard cardiac monitoring and transoesophageal echocardiographic guidance. The previous midline sternotomy was reopened uneventfully with an oscillating saw. Adhesions were lysed, and standard cannulation of the aortic arch and right atrium was performed. A left ventricular vent was placed via the right superior pulmonary vein. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was initiated, and the remainder of the aortic root and ascending aorta was skeletonized.
	We applied an aortic cross-clamp and performed a relatively low and transverse aortotomy to prepare for the eventual distal pulmonary autograft anastomosis. This step allowed the pulmonary autograft to be trimmed as short as possible, at the sinotubular junction (STJ), to remove excess autograft tissue at risk for future aneurysmal dilatation. We delivered ostial del Nido cardioplegia to achieve diastolic arrest and re-dosed at one hour with subsequent doses every 30 minutes.
- 3 - Assessment and explant of the bioprosthetic AV (1:56)
	Three commissural stay sutures were placed to enable adequate exposure. The native STJ was small; thus, we scalloped out the left and right main coronary buttons to provide adequate exposure to the aortic annulus. Upon inspection, we could see the 21-mm Edwards Inspiris bovine pericardial bioprosthesis in situ with the PVL clearly identified (Figure 1b). The valve prosthesis was carefully explanted, along with remnant suture and pledget material (Figure1c). The explanted prosthetic AV appeared to be free of any intrinsic dysfunction.
- 4 - Sizing the left ventricular outflow tract (3:05)
	After full debridement, a 29-mm Freestyle sizer easily fit within the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), suggesting that the annulus was in fact dilated and would require annuloplasty stabilization. We redefined the neo-aortic annulus, taking the well-developed posterior commissure as the baseline and re-creating neo-commissures at 120 degrees from the posterior commissure. The aortic root was circumferentially dissected out externally to the level of the basal ring, in preparation for the aortic annuloplasty sutures. We placed direct coronary ostial catheters for intermittent delivery of cardioplegia.
- 5 - Harvesting and preparing the pulmonary autograft (3:52)
	Although this was the patient’s third sternotomy, the pulmonary autograft was relatively free of adhesions and easy to harvest (Figure 1d). The key was to keep the dissection plane close to the medial aspect of the pulmonary autograft, thereby keeping the left main coronary away and out of sight of the dissection plane. After the body of the pulmonary autograft was exposed, the pulmonary artery was incised distally near the pulmonary bifurcation and transected. The pulmonary valve was examined carefully to ensure that it was a symmetrical tricuspid valve, without major fenestrations or congenital abnormalities that would preclude its use. A right-angled instrument was placed through the pulmonary valve; the right ventricular outflow tract was incised; and the pulmonary autograft was scalloped out of the pulmonary infundibulum, leaving a 2- to 3-mm rim below the cusp insertion. The excised pulmonary autograft was trimmed, and neo-commissures were cut to match the reconstructed aortic annulus to create a symmetrical, tall standing pulmonary autograft when transplanted to the left side of the heart (Figure 2a – 2d).
- 6 - Placement of stabilization sutures for aortic annuloplasty (5:48)
	The aortic annulus neo-commissures were re-checked for commissural symmetry, and the 120-degree stay sutures were replaced more accurately. We then placed six 2-0 ETHIBOND sutures in the subannular plane for the anticipated aortic annuloplasty, with two sutures within each sinus segment, again placed symmetrically (Figure 2b).

	**Figure 2:** **(a)** Pulmonary autograft implant; **(b)** subannular aortic annuloplasty ring; **(c)** completed implantation of the pulmonary autograft; and **(d)** final result intra-operatively.
- 7 - Implanting the pulmonary autograft and repairing the ventricular septal defect (6:12)
	The pulmonary autograft was implanted deeply into the LVOT using interrupted 4-0 Prolene sutures placed radially around the annulus in the following order: right, left and non-coronary cusp sutures. The pulmonary autograft was sunk deep within the LVOT, and all the sutures were tied (Figure 2a). Prior to this step, we identiﬁed a small ventricular septal defect below the membranous septum and closed it primarily with the proximal autograft suture line. The distal end of the pulmonary autograft was trimmed to remove excess pulmonary autograft tissue above the STJ, and three 5-0 Prolene sutures were placed at the top of all three commissures (Figure 2c).
- 8 - Aortic annuloplasty and reimplantation of coronary buttons (7:04)
	We passed the six previously placed stabilization sutures symmetrically through our selected 32-mm Dacron graft for the aortic annuloplasty. The Dacron ring was lowered into place, and the sutures were tied loosely and cut. Then we reimplanted our left main and right main coronary buttons in their respective anatomical positions using running 6-0 Prolene suture. Each coronary button can simply be placed within the centre of the respective sinus without much concern for kinking or distortion because of the pliability of the pulmonary autograft.
- 9 - Preparing and implanting the pulmonary homograft (8:33)
	We previously thawed a 28-mm Artivion SynerGraft pulmonary homograft (Artivion, Kennesaw, GA, USA). We trimmed the distal and proximal ends, marked the homograft orientation and then anastomosed the distal end of the pulmonary bifurcation with two running 5-0 Prolene sutures. The proximal end was anastomosed to the right ventricular outﬂow tract with running 4-0 Prolene sutures. The initial suturing in the pulmonary infundibulum required long, superficial bites to avoid injury to the first septal perforator and avoidance of deep suturing laterally to avoid injury to the left anterior descending artery proper.
- 10 - Attaching the pulmonary autograft to the ascending aorta (10:21)
	We first measured the ascending aorta to ensure that the neo-STJ would be appropriately narrow to stabilize the STJ and prevent splaying of the pulmonary autograft commissures. Then, we carefully attached our pulmonary autograft to the ascending aorta with running 5-0 Prolene sutures. The residual noncoronary sinus and the remnant sinus tissue between the left and right coronary sinus were used to reinforce the distal anastomosis but more importantly to support the pulmonary autograft long term and hopefully prevent future autograft dilatation (Figure 2d).
- 11 - Weaning from CPB and recovery (11:44)
	The patient was gradually weaned from CPB without inotropic support. At the end of the operation, the patient was haemodynamically stable with no inotropic support and was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit in stable condition. Postoperative transoesophageal echocardiography demonstrated excellent function in the pulmonary autograft with mean and peak gradients of 6 and 11 mmHg across the valve with trace aortic insufﬁciency (Figure 3a, 3b). The pulmonary homograft was functioning well with the mean and peak gradients of 1 and 2 mmHg across the valve with no pulmonary insufﬁciency (Figure 3c, 3d). Biventricular function was preserved. The patient had an uncomplicated course in the hospital and was discharged on the postoperative day 5.

- 12 - Outcome & Discussion (13:01)
	**Outcome**
	At one year after the operation, the patient continues to do well with complete resolution of the dyspnoea, is New York Heart Association functional class I and has a well-functioning pulmonary autograft and homograft with normal biventricular function. The patient will be followed for life with annual echocardiograms and MRIs at five-year intervals.
	Discussion
	Whereas mechanical or bioprostheses are commonly chosen across various age groups for AVR, young adults present a unique challenge due to their anticipated longer lifespan, resulting in an increased cumulative risk of prosthetic-related complications. Although bioprostheses do not require long-term anticoagulation, they suffer from limited durability, which results in an increased risk of surgical reintervention. Studies of older patients have reported reintervention rates of up to 30% 10 years after surgery \[5\], which is likely underestimated in younger patients. In contrast, the Ross procedure offers an expected survival rate equivalent to that of age- and sex-matched individuals in the general population. This result is achieved by minimizing prosthetic valve-related complications, avoiding anticoagulation and providing superior haemodynamics \[4, 6\]. However, the wide availability and ease of implanting bioprosthetic valves, as opposed to the more technically demanding Ross procedure, render bioprostheses an attractive option. This finding is especially true in centres with little expertise in the Ross procedure and for patients who may not be optimal candidates for the procedure.
	In experienced centres, the Ross procedure has a perioperative mortality and major morbidity risk comparable to that of conventional AVR \[4\]. Moreover, a landmark randomized controlled trial comparing the Ross procedure with homograft aortic root replacement revealed superior survival rates in adults who underwent the Ross procedure. Furthermore, the Ross cohort experienced increased freedom from reoperation and reported enhanced quality of life \[7\]. These results may be attributed to the pulmonary autograft’s status as a living structure, enabling it to undergo remodelling and to adapt to haemodynamic changes. They hold particular significance for younger adult patients who lead active lifestyles, because their heightened haemodynamic requirements are effectively met by the pulmonary autograft’s near-physiological performance.
	To achieve success with the Ross procedure, a customized approach based on patient characteristics is imperative. Over time, refinements in technique have been introduced to enhance outcomes. At our institution, we prioritize preserving sufficient native aortic wall, including the non-coronary sinus and the bridge between the left and right sinuses. This preserved tissue is later utilized in the anastomosis with the ascending aorta, providing external support for the pulmonary autograft. Additionally, we have learned to implant the autograft deep within the LVOT using a full root technique, ensuring effective stabilization of the annulus and the proximal end of the autograft. Moreover, as part of our standard practice, we perform external aortic annuloplasty with a Dacron graft ring for annular diameters of 27 mm or larger or in patients with significant aortic regurgitation to mitigate the risk of late dilation and recurrent aortic insufficiency \[4\].
	Performing a Ross procedure in patients having a re-operation presents notable technical challenges due to a commonly present ‘frozen aortic root’, significant aortic annular distortion or destruction, managing adhesions and scarring and potential complications. The most demanding aspect of these re-operations involves navigating through adhesions and scar tissue during the explantation of previous aortic root grafts. After complete root debridement, the subsequent steps closely resemble those of a standard Ross procedure. In re-operation scenarios, the pulmonary autograft typically is not adhered, making its removal akin to a primary operation. Exceptions occur when the pulmonary autograft is fused with a previous Dacron aortic root graft or aortic homograft, where removing both together and separating them afterward may be necessary. Regardless of the choice of procedure—Ross, biological or mechanical—one should note that re-operation risks cannot be eliminated. After complete debridement, careful decision making, judgement and experience ultimately guide best reconstructive options. In experienced centres, performing a Ross procedure in re-operation may extend surgical time but usually does not significantly increase perioperative risks compared to other aortic root replacement options.
	As specialized centres see a resurgence in performing the Ross procedure, new evidence continues to emerge, supporting its efficacy as an option for AVR. However, it is important to note that, while we uphold our belief in the superior long-term outcomes of the pulmonary autograft in young adult patients, the supporting evidence originates largely from very experienced single- and multicentre cohort observational studies, and questions about the generalizability of this specialized operation remain paramount. Moreover, there is only one randomized controlled trial in this area, further underscoring the need for additional research to solidify our understanding of the Ross procedure’s benefits in this patient demographic and in higher risk re-operations.
	Follow-up at one year
	The patient was asymptomatic with a well-functioning pulmonary autograft and homograft and normal biventricular function.
