The Ross procedure remains the procedure of choice for managing unrepairable aortic valves, especially in young adults and those with active lifestyles. However, the Achilles heel of this procedure has been aortic root dilation and the potential need for reoperation that may be associated with increased risks in addition to the need for intervention on the pulmonary outflow tract.

Over the years, this procedure underwent several modifications to improve long-term durability and decrease the rate of reinterventions.

Modifying the Ross procedure by including an autograft inside a Dacron graft seems to have the potential advantage of stabilizing the autograft diameter, which may be associated with improved durability and decrease the need for a future reoperation.

Another limitation to the widespread application of this procedure is related to the use of homografts in the reconstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract. The cost and the availability of the homografts continue to be limiting factors and, with the recent surge in the number of Ross procedures, other options for reconstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract must be considered.

We present three additional options for homografts that we have successfully utilized as part of the Ross procedure during reconstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract.

Different techniques for reconstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) have been reported in the literature with accompanying long-term data \[1\]. Although the pathological issue can be different, the technical aspects of implanting and reconstructing the RVOT are consistent and reproducible.

Various options are available but have not been utilized in the setting of the Ross procedure, apart from some recent reports of the use of hand-made polytetrafluoroethylene conduits for RVOT reconstruction with both bicuspid and tricuspid valve versions \[2\].

We present four different options for RVOT reconstruction that were used in young adult patients: (i) standard pulmonary homograft, (ii) hand-made valved conduit with a bioprosthesis inside a Dacron graft, (iii) a xenograft (Freestyle valve, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and (iv) autologous reconstruction of a new trileaflet pulmonary valve using the patient’s own pericardium (pulmonary Ozaki) \[3, 4\].

The technique utilized for harvesting the pulmonary autograft and its inclusion inside the Dacron graft are the same in all patients, and the focus is on the technical aspects of RVOT reconstruction with the above-mentioned methods.

- 1 - Patient Presentation (0:13)
	We discuss four different patients who presented with symptomatic severe aortic valve (AV) stenosis secondary to congenital AV stenosis.
	Standard preoperative evaluation included a transthoracic echocardiogram, a cardiac computed tomography angiography scan to evaluate the aorta and determine the need for concomitant replacement of the ascending aorta.
	Suitability of the pulmonary valve (PV) for the Ross procedure is determined based on a combination of preoperative and intraoperative factors. A transoesophageal echocardiogram is added to further evaluate the AV intraoperatively and confirms the suitability of the PV as well.
- 2 - Preparing and implanting the pulmonary autograft in the left ventricular outflow tract (1:25)
	A median sternotomy is performed. In the case for which a pulmonary Ozaki procedure is planned, a large sheet is harvested from the anterior pericardium, being aware of the location of the phrenic nerves. This sheet is then treated with glutaraldehyde 0.6% for 10 minutes and then rinsed three times in saline, each for 6 minutes. This sheet is used later to reconstruct the neo-PV leaflets in a manner similar to that used to construct the AV leaflets in the standard Ozaki procedure.
	Heparin is then administered systemically, followed by proximal aortic arch and bicaval cannulation. Once the activated clotting time is satisfactory, cardiopulmonary bypass is initiated in the standard fashion, and the core temperature is brought down to 34°Celsius. A left ventricular vent is placed through the right superior pulmonary vein.
	The main pulmonary artery (PA) is then transected, and the PV is inspected to determine its suitability for the Ross procedure. Once it is deemed suitable, we start harvesting the pulmonary autograft on a beating heart. Using a combination of electrocautery and the scissors, the pulmonary autograft is harvested without difficulty. Attention is given to the location of the first septal perforator during this process to avoid injuring it. This strategy significantly reduces the ischaemic time. The bed of the harvested autograft is inspected for any bleeding, which is addressed at this stage to avoid cumbersome bleeding at the end of the procedure because these areas are difficult to reach at the completion of the procedure.
	The autograft is then trimmed and sized with a Hegar dilator. The reinforcing Dacron graft size is then determined; it is usually 4 mm larger than the proximal diameter of the autograft. The autograft is then secured proximally and distally inside the Dacron graft using three running 5/0 Prolene sutures at each end. The commissures of the autograft are aligned with the markings on the Dacron graft to maintain orientation and avoid any distortion later during implantation.
	One manoeuvre that can be helpful is eversion of the Dacron graft after securing the proximal end of the autograft. This step is followed by securing the distal end and then returning the autograft back to its original state. This procedure allows proper testing of the autograft valve under pressure and saves one distal suture line if concomitant replacement of the ascending aorta is needed.
	The autograft valve is inspected and tested with saline.
	An ascending aorta cardioplegia needle is then placed, followed by application of the aortic cross-clamp. Cardioplegic arrest is achieved with antegrade cardioplegia. The ascending aorta is then transected, and the dilated portion is resected. Stay sutures are placed at the aortic root, followed by harvesting of the left and the right coronary artery buttons. These buttons are mobilized off the epicardial surface of the heart with electrocautery. The diseased AV leaflets are resected, and the annulus is debrided. The reinforced autograft is then implanted in the native aortic root using running 4/0 Prolene sutures. Three sutures are used for this part of the procedure. Attention is paid especially during securement of the anterior part of the autograft to avoid distortion or injury to the autograft valve.
	The left, followed by the right, coronary artery buttons are then implanted in the middle of the corresponding sinuses of Valsalva of the autograft. The implant procedure includes all three layers (the native coronary button, the Dacron graft and the autograft wall) and is completed using running 5/0 Prolene sutures.
	One additional tip is that the right coronary artery button can be implanted after completion of the posterior suture line of the distal aortic anastomosis, which serves to stabilize the aortic root and prevent its rotation, which may compromise the proximal right coronary artery especially in overweight or obese patients in whom the epicardial fat on top of the right ventricle can be a contributing factor.
	In all patients but one, the aortic reconstruction is completed prior to reconstructing the RVOT, which depends on whether or not the distal anastomosis for the PA can be easily visualized after completion of the aortic reconstruction. After the aortic reconstruction is completed, the cardioplegia needle is placed back in the Dacron graft. This way we can pressurize the autograft and test the neo-AV appropriately. This test shows whether the autograft valve exhibits satisfactory competence and also checks for any bleeding at the root level.
- 3 - Homograft reconstruction of the RVOT (5:40)
	This step is done after the aortic cross-clamp is removed. We usually select a large cryopreserved pulmonary homograft for reconstructing the RVOT. The distal anastomosis to the pulmonary arterial confluence is done first using running 5/0 Prolene suture.
	The proximal homograft to the RVOT anastomosis is then completed with a running 4/0 Prolene suture. The suture line can be supported with a strip of either the autologous pericardium or the Dacron graft to ensure haemostasis.
- 4 - Freestyle xenograft reconstruction of the RVOT (6:59)
	The freestyle prosthesis represents another alternative for RVOT reconstruction; however, because it is a bit short, we use a short segment of a Dacron graft to bridge that gap. The Dacron graft is sewn to the pulmonary arterial confluence using running 5/0 Prolene suture. The freestyle prosthesis is sewn to the RVOT with a running 4/0 Prolene suture. This suture line can also be supported with a strip of autologous pericardium or Dacron graft to ensure haemostasis. Then the distal freestyle-to-Dacron graft connection is reestablished.
	Due to the relative rigidity of the freestyle prosthesis, the proximal anastomosis to the RVOT can be done while the heart is still arrested.
- 5 - Hand-made valved conduit reconstruction of the RVOT (9:15)
	An appropriately sized Dacron graft is anastomosed to the pulmonary arterial confluence using running 5/0 Prolene suture. Then the proximal end is bevelled and its length is adjusted.
	A standard aortic bioprosthesis is secured inside the proximal graft with a running 4/0 Prolene suture. The bevelled end of the Dacron graft is then sewn to the right ventricle using running 4/0 Prolene suture. It is not necessary to include the sewing ring of the bioprosthesis in the suture line.
- 6 - Reconstruction of a tricuspid neo-pulmonary valve with autologous pericardium: the pulmonary Ozaki (10:36)
	The continuity of the right ventricular-to-pulmonary arterial confluence is re-established using two separate pieces of the same Dacron graft. It is secured distally to the branch pulmonary arteries using running 5/0 Prolene sutures and proximally to the native autograft bed using running 4/0 Prolene suture. The heart is then de-aired, and the aortic cross-clamp is removed. A vent is placed through the right atrium into the right ventricle to improve visualization.
	We use the Ozaki AV sizers to determine the size of the neo-PV leaflets. In the current case, we create three leaflets of equal size—each is 29 mm.
	The leaflets are cut from the autologous pericardial sheet that was harvested and prepared at the beginning of the case.
	The inside of the proximal RVOT Dacron graft is then marked, and the locations of the neo-commissures are determined. Leaflets are then sewn using running 4/0 Prolene suture. This part can be done on a beating heart.
	Three neo-commissures are then created. The PV leaflets are reconstructed using the same procedure as that used to perform the Ozaki procedure for the AV.
	The two RVOT Dacron grafts are then sewn together using running 4/0 Prolene suture to complete the reconstruction of the neo-PA.
- 7 - All Together (14:02)
	The patient is weaned off CPB. Once the post-bypass transoesophageal echocardiogram confirms satisfactory surgical results, the patient is decannulated, and heparin is reversed with protamine. The rest of the procedure is concluded in the standard fashion.
	The four different techniques of reconstruction of the RVOT in the Ross procedure are shown.

All patients did well postoperatively and during their follow-up periods. No interventions were required to any of the RVOT conduits during the one-year follow-up period.

Several modifications were performed to the current procedures to minimize the ischaemic time, to obtain the best haemodynamics and to ensure long-term durability. Harvesting the pulmonary autograft on a beating heart \[5\] decreases the ischaemic time significantly and allows inclusion of the autograft in the Dacron graft (autograft reinforcement) without adding to the aortic cross-clamp time.

We believe in reinforcing the pulmonary autograft in adult patients because it stabilizes the autograft and prevents future dilation, which may have the potential to minimize/avoid reoperation.

To expand the utilization of the Ross procedure, alternate options for RVOT reconstruction have to be considered. The different techniques that we demonstrated in the current report are important especially in parts of the world where homografts cannot be found. The four techniques described are well established in the congenital literature, and each has its own source of long-term data, albeit in different anatomical/pathological settings. They are also very reproducible.

However, an important question remains that is related to the long-term outcomes of these alternate techniques for RVOT reconstruction compared to the use of a homograft in the setting of the Ross procedure.
