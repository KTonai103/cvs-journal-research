Gradual dilatation of the neo-aortic/pulmonary root or development of an autograft aneurysm and associated valve regurgitation is a major fear and a serious late complication after the Ross procedure to preserve the “principle of a living valve” after the Ross operation by performing the valve-sparing reimplantation technique (David procedure). This article addresses the main peculiarities of this redo scenario compared to a primary/standard David procedure.

This video tutorial shows the valve-sparing reimplantation technique used in a patient with neo-aortic root dilatation and concomitant neo-aortic valve regurgitation arising late after the Ross procedure, emphasising the main differences in the operative technique in relation to the primary/standard David procedure.

- 1 - Patient Presentation (0:13)
	The patient is a 35-year-old, 65-kg female with a neo-aortic root dilatation and concomitant significant neo-aortic valve regurgitation late after a free-standing autograft replacement (Ross procedure; for right ventricular pulmonary artery reconstruction, a cryopreserved homograft was implanted). The Ross procedure was performed when the patient was 15 years old because she had a symptomatic stenotic bicuspid aortic valve. She did well for 20 years, after which she began to suffer from progressive episodes of palpitations during exercise, lastly at rest.
- 2 - Preoperative imaging (0:21)
	A diagnostic work-up using transthoracic echocardiography revealed significant central neo-aortic valve regurgitation (pressure half-time 380 ms, vena contracta 7 mm) and an aneurysmal neo-aortic root (neo-aortic root diameter, 54 mm). The neo-aortic annulus measured 26 mm in diameter. All three cusps were pliable and without any calcifications or sclerosis. The left ventricular ejection fraction was 61%, and the left ventricular end diastolic diameter was 62 mm. The formerly implanted right ventricular pulmonary artery conduit (homograft) showed good haemodynamics without regurgitation or stenosis. A coronary angiogram confirmed the non-existence of coronary artery disease.
- 3 - Re-sternotomy and dissection of the heart and great vessels (1:04)
	A median re-sternotomy and sternal re-entry were performed. Dissection and preparation of all relevant cardiac structures were carried out.
- 4 - Establishment of extracorporeal circulation and preparation and dissection of the neo-aortic root and pulmonary artery (1:31)
	Arterial and venous cannulation was done in the standard fashion. For optimal myocardial protection, a cardioplegic solution (Bretschneider solution) was administered in an ante- and retrograde fashion by indirect placement of a catheter via the right atrium into the coronary sinus. Extensive dissection between the neo-aortic wall and the right ventricle/pulmonary root was performed in preparation for the proper placement of horizontal U stitches.
- 5 - Mobilization and assessment of the neo-aortic root and the coronary artery buttons (2:02)
	After the ascending aorta was transected, both coronary arteries and neo-aortic sinuses were resected. The main difference compared to a primary David procedure was the extensive circumferential mobilization of the neo-aortic root to a level well below that of the neo-aortic annulus. An additional cornerstone of a successful David procedure in the redo setting constitutes a meticulous separation and preparation of the right ventricular outflow tract and aortic root. These steps could be performed safely in the setting of a full-root Ross procedure without a greater surgical effort. Dissection and preparation of the neo-aortic root after a jacket or wrapped Ross procedure might be much more challenging due to denser adhesions.
- 6 - Placement of neo-aortic subvalvular sutures and assessment of the vascular graft size (3:42)
	Horizontal U stitches were placed circumferentially beneath the neo-aortic annulus using Teflon felts. To address the large amount of tissue around the aortic root, it is advisable to use a needle with a greater curve to prevent distortion and retraction of the neo-aortic valve cusps. To assess the vascular graft size, a Hegar dilator was used to measure the diameter of the neo-aortic annulus.
- 7 – Down-sliding of the vascular graft and knotting of the basal sutures (5:10)
	The vascular prosthesis is parachuted down to the neo-aortic annulus, and knotting of the Teflon felts is accomplished.
- 8 - Adjustment and resuspension of the commissures and neo-aortic valve cusps within the vascular graft (5:24)
	The commissures are positioned inside the vascular graft by creating a symmetrical orientation of the cusps without billowing.
- 9 - Neo-aortic valve-sparing reimplantation by means of a proximal suture line (5:46)
	The free margins of the neo-aortic sinuses are sutured into the vascular graft in a continuous fashion starting from the nadirs and proceeding towards the peaks of the commissures.
- 10 - Reimplantation of the coronary artery buttons (6:42)
	The left and right coronary arteries were reimplanted using a running suture technique.
- 11 - Distal anastomosis and situs after decannulation (8:07)
	The distal anastomosis was completed under echocardiographic-guided de-airing of the left heart structures. The heart was beating with adequate haemostasis following de-airing.
- 12 - Postoperative imaging (8:32)
	The patient was extubated five hours after the operation. Before she was discharged, transthoracic echocardiography revealed superior haemodynamics for the autograft valve (pmax 17 mmHg; pmean 9 mmHg; trace autograft valve regurgitation) as well as the formerly implanted homograft (pmax 16 mmHg; pmean 8 mmHg; a minimal homograft regurgitation) and excellent functioning of the right and left ventricles. The patient spent one night in the intensive care unit and was discharged without complications on postoperative day seven.
- 13 - Postoperative course (8:56)
	- Uneventful
	- Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography: EF 60%, autograft with trace AR, AV pmax17 mmHg, pmean9 mmHg, homograft (RV-PA-conduit) minimal regurgitation
	- Discharge at 7 <sup>th</sup> postoperative day

Outcome

**Discussion**

Progressive neo-sinus dilatation remains a major limitation after the Ross procedure \[1,2\]. This case indicates that the David procedure can be safely and effectively performed to spare a dilated pulmonary autograft valve (initially performed using a full-root technique) in patients requiring autograft reoperations \[3–5\].
