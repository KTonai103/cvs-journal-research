ABSTRACT

BACKGROUND Minimally invasive cardiac surgery (MICS) has become a popular approach due to its
potential benefits, such as improved cosmesis, faster recovery, shorter hospital stays, and costeffectiveness, compared with traditional median sternotomy. However, there have been some
concerns regarding procedural efficiency and surgical outcomes, especially in the early phase of the
learning curve of these procedures.
METHODS In March 2025, a systematic review was conducted using MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane
Library and Google Scholar databases to identify potential studies that quantitively assessed the
learning curve in MICS using predefined metrics based on surgical times and/or clinical outcomes.
RESULTS There were 28 studies involving 13,257 patients that met the inclusion criteria, most of
which were retrospective, focusing on 3 types of MICS: minimally invasive mitral valve surgery, aortic
valve replacement, and coronary artery bypass grafting. The learning curve was assessed using
arbitrary (split-group) and nonarbitrary (cumulative sum) methods. Common perioperative metrics
included operative, cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic cross-clamp times, and postoperative complications. The reported number of cases needed to overcome the learning curve varied widely, ranging
from 23 to 125 cases (mean, 39 cases [for repair] and 78 [for replacement]) for minimally invasive
valve surgery, 40 to 138 cases (mean, 93 cases) for minimally invasive aortic valve replacement, and
16 to 100 cases (mean, 40 cases) for minimally invasive coronary artery bypass grafting.
CONCLUSIONS Differences in surgical process and postoperative outcomes suggest a learning curve
in MICS, although stable morbidity and mortality rates indicate the safe adoption of these procedures with appropriate training. Nonetheless, significant heterogeneity across studies prevents
precise learning curve characterization, highlighting the need for standardized, multivariable
assessment frameworks.

of the learning curve in nonrobotic MICS and
examines the number of cases required to overcome the initial learning phase. One concern we
identified is the wide variability in the number of
procedures required to overcome the learning
curve, as reported NLC figures ranged from as low
as 23 to as high as 138 in some types of MICS. The

For example, early-career surgeons or those in
lower-volume centers may require significantly
more operative exposure to reach proficiency,
adding another factor that must be accounted
for when interpreting the learning curve.43,44
Another factor to consider is interprocedural
differences, because certain types of MICS are
more technically challenging for the surgical
team than others,45 which manifests as a higher
NLC range. This reflects the importance of
procedure-specific learning curve assessments.
The study by Davierwala and colleagues23 reported
that their outcomes statistically decreased after
1330 MICABG cases; however, this number
represents their experience during the first 7-year
period of the study rather than focusing on outcomes during the initial learning curve phase.
Of note, postoperative complications, such as
mortality and reoperation for bleeding, did not
significantly improve in the later phase of any
type of MICS featured in our meta-analysis. We
hypothesize this is due to the buffering effect of
institutional safeguards. Specifically, institutionwide postoperative protocols, patient monitoring efforts, or the implementation of perioperative care teams may have helped to reduce
complications in the early learning phase. Existing literature also urges clinicians to use extensive training series when dealing with
dichotomous rare events to model the learning
curve more accurately.46 It was also argued that
the use of intraoperative metrics may be
unreliable for assessment of surgical competency
and may poorly represent surgical skill.8 It was
proposed that instead, a multivariable approach
should be used to cover for the weaknesses of
other metrics and provide a holistic measure of
competency.8
The use of CUSUM and its variations has
remained the most robust strategy for assessing
and tangibly quantifying the learning curve in
MICS. Arbitrary-based approaches have been
shown numerous times in literature to have
noteworthy limitations, because consecutive
cases are often stratified with no consistent
rationale for splitting points.8,47 Besides, this
method is only effective with larger sample
sizes,8,47 and even then, the exact NLC range
becomes tricky to ascertain, as seen in the

These findings highlight the need for standardization in how learning curves are measured
and reported. The systematic review by Harrysson and colleagues49 reported a framework for
learning curves in minimally invasive abdominal
surgery and can be summarized into 4 elements:
(1) using multiple clearly defined outcome
variables, (2) featuring various institutions and
surgeons, (3) performing a multivariate analysis
that accounts for different surgeons, teams, and
patients, and lastly (4) including a graphical
display of data. Similarly, Kuemmerli and
colleagues50 proposed a standardized 3-phase
reporting of learning phases (competency, proficiency, and mastery), stressing the need to

In addition, evidence-based educational strategies are essential for optimizing learning and
minimizing complications during early MICS
adoption. Notably, practice-based training regimens have gained popularity recently for being a
viable solution to the ethical conundrum of surgical teams learning new technically complex
procedures on actual patients.51 They do not
directly substitute real-time learning but are
rather integrated into the training curriculum for
those interested in practicing MICS for a holistic
experience.51,52 This allows motor skills to be
developed in a controlled, safe setting that will
not compromise patient safety.8,51 Previous
literature has also demonstrated that the
incorporated extrinsic performance-based feedback by an expert, in a limited fashion and
particularly after a motor task, promotes faster
technical skill acquisition and overall safer transition to clinical work.51
A preliminary high-fidelity MIMVS simulator has
already been designed and tested by 99 surgeons.53
The consensus was that the simulator is a
promising method for training MIMVS techniques
that accurately mimic patient-specific anatomy.53
Since then, numerous studies corroborated that
surgical residents trained with high-fidelity simulators experienced a noticeable decrease in

Understanding the learning curve for MICS can
have significant clinical implications at multiple
levels. From the patient’s perspective, knowing
the team’s performance metrics and comfort with
MICS can improve the informed consent process.
Also, understanding the learning curve can help
optimize patient selection and ensure a tailored
surgical approach.44 From a hospital’s managerial
perspective, understanding the anticipated
learning process associated with a new MICS
program can provide realistic data on program
progress and growth expectations.56 Finally, it
is important to mention the cumulative effort
from all members of the surgical team, including
anesthesiologists, intensivists, perfusionists, and
nursing staff. Therefore, investing in departmentwide training and education is key to achieving
exceptional standards of care.
Of note, robotically assisted MICS has gained
popularity among different cardiac surgical units
worldwide. Studies assessing the learning for robotic procedures similarly showed heterogeneity
and lack of standardization in learning curve
methods and measures used. In the case of
robotically assisted MICABG, literature shows NLC
figures ranging between 10 cases to attain competency57 up to 200 to 500 for complete mastery,58
with 50 cases as the point when complication rates
decline significantly.59 Additionally, robotically
assisted MICS is associated with a steep initial
learning curve due to technical and logistical
complexity of the procedure.59,60 For instance,
some of the challenges during initial training
may include mastering robotic internal mammary
artery harvesting, performing anastomoses deep
within the chest through a small incision, safe
port placement, management of peripheral CPB,
and myocardial protection.59,61
Literature comparing the learning curve of robotic vs nonrobotic MICS head-to-head is scarce
and not possible due to inherent differences between the 2 techniques. Nonetheless, several
studies using propensity score matching have
demonstrated that robotic and nonrobotic MICS
yield broadly comparable safety profiles, with
consistent and statistically significant differences
in key intraoperative variables and postoperative
outcomes. For example, robotic-assisted MICS
procedures are often associated with longer
operative times62 but tend to offer shorter

LIMITATIONS. First, the included studies exhibited

significant heterogeneity in study design, criteria
used to describe the point of overcoming the
learning curve, patient populations, variations in
the definitions of key terms, such as “technical
proficiency/competence” or “surgical failure,”
and surgical techniques (across and within the
same surgical subtypes). Our meta-analysis was
limited to intraoperative and postoperative
metrics and only a limited fraction of variables
could be analyzed across a limited number of
studies, and oftentimes, not all MICS subtypes
were represented.
Another limitation can be attributed to the
retrospective design of included papers and the
fact that they were single-center investigations.
This reduces the clinical reproducibility and
generalizability of any finding because of
inherent biases and unaccounted confounding
factors, including previous surgical experience.

Finally, although efforts were made to assess the

CONCLUSION. Differences in metrics based on sur-

gical times and operative outcomes demonstrate
the presence of a learning curve in MICS. However, the lack of statistical differences in morbidity
and mortality rates during the initial phases of the
learning process may indicate that these procedures can be performed safely with proper training
and mentoring. Owing to the significant heterogeneity among the included studies, it remains
difficult to accurately characterize and quantify
this learning curve. Therefore, future studies
should implement a multivariable and standardized approach for the assessment and reporting of
learning curve metrics to mitigate its potential
impact on early outcomes after MICS.
