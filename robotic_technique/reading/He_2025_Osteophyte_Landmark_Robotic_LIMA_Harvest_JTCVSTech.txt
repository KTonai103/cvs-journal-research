ABSTRACT
Objective: Robotic left internal mammary artery (LIMA) harvesting can present
various challenges for novice surgeons. This study aimed to investigate whether
the osteophyte of the ﬁrst costochondral joint (OFCCJ) could serve as an anatomical landmark for robotic LIMA harvesting and to explore the optimal standardized
process.
Methods: The concept of “LIMA isthmus” was described on the basis of relatively
ﬁxed anatomical relationship between LIMA and OFCCJ, and the height of OFCCJ
and its distance to LIMA were deﬁned. The presence and features of OFCCJ were
analyzed in 514 hospitalized patients with or without coronary artery diseases (CAD
or non-CAD). The value of “LIMA isthmus”-based robotic harvesting strategy was
further assessed for beginners during the learning curve phase.
Results: The OFCCJs were found to be very common in the adult population. The
prevalence of OFCCJ in patients with CAD was signiﬁcantly greater than in patients
without CAD (73.16% vs 60.33%, P< .01). However, there were no signiﬁcant difference between the CAD and the non-CAD group in the height of the OFCCJ or the
distance between the OFCCJ and the LIMA (15.54  4.99 mm vs 16.55  4.70 mm,
P ¼ .06; 12.66  4.19 mm vs 12.45  6.87 mm, P ¼ .72). During the learning curve
phase, the time to identify LIMA and the time for LIMA harvesting using “LIMA
isthmus”-based strategy were signiﬁcantly shorter than those with conventional
approach (89.00  31.07 seconds vs 1087  436.1 seconds, P ¼ .035;
86.80  4.74 minutes vs 110.5  11.72 minutes, P ¼ .013). The inadvertent bleeding
events with “LIMA isthmus”-based graft harvesting were much fewer (1.00  0.19
vs 3.44  0.34, P<.001), although there was no difference between 2 beginners in
postoperative chest drainage, LIMA injury events, or conversion to a median
sternotomy incision.
Conclusions: OFCCJ could serve as a reliable anatomical landmark to guide robotic
LIMA harvesting. “LIMA isthmus”-based standardized robotic LIMA harvesting
could signiﬁcantly reduce the time to determine LIMA and the time for graft harvesting process, and minimize the risk of inadvertent bleeding events. This could
be particularly beneﬁcial for novice surgeons during the initial learning curve phase
of this procedure. (JTCVS Techniques 2025;29:56-64)

METHODS
Concept of “LIMA Isthmus”
The LIMA is typically located beneath the chest wall fascia, with its
outline and pulsation vaguely visible in the upper third of its course.

Adult: Coronary

Surrounding anatomical structures, such as the pericardium, left pulmonary
hilum, left phrenic nerve, and left subclavian artery, can be observed along
its course. However, in a considerable number of patients, the presence of
mediastinal fat or overdeveloped transversus thoracis muscle within the
chest wall can completely obscure the LIMA and its surrounding structures, which makes the localization of the LIMA relatively challenging
and potentially results in inadvertent damage to the LIMA during
harvesting.
In our previous practice of robotic LIMA harvesting, we noted that
prominent bony protrusions often appear at the first costochondral joint,
also called OFCCJ, regardless of the amount of mediastinal fat, and that
the LIMA is always situated within the isthmus between this bony protrusion and the aortic arch, with the phrenic nerve crossing over the LIMA
(Figure 1). This relatively fixed anatomical relationship seems to provide
a stable reference point for locating LIMA. On the basis of this observation,
we defined the confined regional structures as the “LIMA isthmus,” which
is the narrow connection between the OFCCJ and the aortic arch, through
which LIMA cross. This concept may facilitate locating LIMA quickly and
protect LIMA and phrenic nerve from inadvertent injury.

Patients
The study population comprised 514 hospitalized patients who underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest at the Cardiovascular Disease Center of Daping Hospital between July 2016 and June
2024. Of these, 272 patients were diagnosed with coronary artery disease
(CAD), and 242 individuals did not have CAD, both confirmed by coronary
artery angiography or computed tomography screening. In addition, relevant clinical data, such as age, gender, height, weight, body mass index,
and the presence of bone metabolism-affecting diseases (eg, diabetes mellitus, thyroid and parathyroid disorders, autoimmune diseases, renal insufficiency, and tumors), as well as the use of specific medications (eg,
glucocorticoids), were collected for all participants.

Evaluation and Characterization of OFCCJ
Two independent researchers were tasked with analyzing and documenting the presence and features of OFCCJ: (1) determine the presence
of OFCCJ; (2) measure the maximum height of the OFCCJ; and (3) identify
the closest distance from the OFCCJ to the LIMA. The detailed methodology for this analysis is outlined as follows (Figure 2). The first step involved
assessing the presence of OFCCJ on CT scans of the chest. Typically, on the
transverse section of the CT scan of the chest at the anterior end of the first
rib, there is a nonsmooth pleural line extending from posterior to anterior,
with visible bony protrusions. The second step focused on identifying the
optimal section for measuring the OFCCJ on CT scans of the chest. By
locating the first costochondral joint (where the sternum, left clavicular
head, and the anterior end of the first rib are simultaneously visible), multiple sections were examined to identify the section with the most prominent bone spur for subsequent determination and measurement. The third
step was measuring the height of the bone spur. A line was drawn along
the arcuate chest wall at the attachment point of the bone spur; the distance
from this line to the highest point of the bone spur defined the height of the
OFCCJ. In the fourth step, the distance between the OFCCJ and LIMA was
measured, specifically the closest distance between the top of the bone spur
identified in the third step and the LIMA.

Anatomical Features of OFCCJ and Its Spatial
Relationship With LIMA
To assess the feasibility of using the OFCCJ as a landmark for robotic LIMA harvesting, the anatomical features
of OFFCJ and its spatial relationship with LIMA were
investigated. Analysis of CT images revealed no significant
difference in the height of the OFCCJ between patients with
CAD and those without. The average height of the OFCCJ
in the CAD group was 15.54  4.99 mm (Table 2). This
consistent and pronounced bony protrusion would facilitate
the rapid identification of the surrounding structures under
the high magnification of the robot’s field of view.
Furthermore, the distance between the OFCCJ and the
LIMA, which reflects the reliability and convenience of

using the OFCCJ as a landmark for LIMA localization,
did not differ significantly between the 2 groups. The
average distance between the OFCCJ and LIMA in the
patients with CAD was 12.66  4.19 mm. The fat tissues
overlying the OFCCJ could be easily dissected and flipped,
allowing for the expedient exposure of the proximal portion
of the LIMA.

approaches have been described, including pedicled, skeletonized, and semiskeletonized techniques. The benefits of

The use of the da Vinci Xi robotic system has demonstrated significant advantages in MICS CABG. Because
of its exceptional image stability, reduced unintentional
smearing of the thoracoscope, and high flexibility and
maneuverability, the da Vinci Xi robot can effectively access both the proximal and distal ends of the LIMA,
enabling precise dissection. Therefore, the da Vinci Xi
robot is exceptionally well-suited for skeletonized LIMA
harvesting. However, the adoption of robotic CABG has
been relatively low, with only approximately 1% of coronary bypass procedures in the United States being
completed with robotic assistance.12 One of the primary
reasons for the limited uptake of robotic CABG is the
considerable challenges faced by novice surgeons. These
difficulties include the inability to accurately identify the
mediastinal anatomy under the surgical scope, as well as unfamiliarity with the manipulation of robotic instruments.
Specifically, the rapid identification of the LIMA and the
precise skeletonized harvesting of the graft are the most
prominent problems, especially when the LIMA is obscured
by thick fat or muscular tissue. In this study, surgeon A
spent between 23 and 70 minutes to identify the LIMA in
the initial 4 cases, and the overall time required for graft
harvesting was significantly prolonged, highlighting the
steep learning curve associated with this procedure. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop a standardized
method that can efficiently and reliably determine the location of the LIMA and facilitate its skeletonized harvesting.
In the study, we found that OFCCJ was a common and
prominent anatomical feature in most adult cases. Furthermore, the LIMA was observed to be about 10 mm away
from the OFCCJ, situated within the narrow groove between the OFCCJ and the aortic arch, as viewed through
the surgical perspective of a robotic scope. Consequently,
we proposed the concept of the “LIMA isthmus” and the
OFCCJ as reliable landmarks for locating the LIMA. On
the basis of this concept, we further standardized the process of robotic skeletonized LIMA harvesting, which
enhanced the precision and efficiency of the subsequent
dissection steps. By carefully removing the soft tissue
covering the LIMA, the entire course of the vessel was
clearly delineated, and then the LIMA was unroofed. The
inferior side of the LIMA could then be dissected rapidly,
with minimal branch arteries encountered, thereby
providing the LIMA with some mobility. The superior
side of the LIMA was then dissected precisely, exposing
the major LIMA branches, which were subsequently ligated
with titanium clips or cauterized.

Robotic LIMA harvesting has a reported learning curve of
approximately 10-20 cases.13,14 To further evaluate the efficacy of this novel harvesting technique, we compared the
LIMA harvesting outcomes of the initial 10 cases performed
by 2 surgeons using different harvesting methods. The
“LIMA isthmus”-based harvesting strategy demonstrated significant improvements in technical simplicity, LIMA harvesting time, graft quality, and reduced bleeding events during the
learning curve phase. Oehlinger and colleagues13 reported
their experiences with robotic LIMA harvesting in 100 cases,
noting a decrease in median harvesting time from 140 minutes
in the first 10 cases to 34 minutes in the last 10 cases. Similarly, Fujita and colleagues15 found no significant difference
in harvesting time between pedicled (21 cases) and skeletonized (12 cases) techniques, with a mean time of
68  13 minutes. However, they reported 3 cases requiring
conversion to standard sternotomy as the result of severe adhesions between the LIMA and the second rib, a difficulty that
appeared to be mitigated by the “LIMA isthmus”-based
approach. Masroor and colleagues6 reported a mean LIMA
harvesting time of 164.7  51.9 minutes, slightly longer
than other studies and our own findings. Although there is
no significant difference in postoperative chest drainage between the 2 groups, we observed a significant difference in
bleeding events from the LIMA and its branches during the
LIMA harvesting process. It is crucial to emphasize that
bleeding events from the LIMA and its branches inevitably
lead to additional hemostatic procedures and potential damage to the LIMA graft vessel, which may affect the longterm patency rates of the LIMA grafts. Interestingly, Fujita
and colleagues15 initially used a pedicled harvesting technique and then transitioned to a skeletonized approach,
whereas Masroor and colleagues6 shifted from a skeletonized
to a pedicled technique. These contrasting technique shifts
across different surgical teams suggest the potential value of
the “LIMA isthmus”-based standardized harvesting strategy,
particularly in the initial stage of the learning curve.
Study Limitations
The primary limitation of this study is that there were
nearly 27% of patients with CAD lacking an OFCCJ. However, the concept of the “LIMA isthmus” could still be used
to rapidly identify the LIMA between the aortic arch and the
anterior aspect of the first rib, rather than relying on the
OFCCJ. In addition, the study only evaluated the utility of
the “LIMA isthmus”-based robotic harvesting approach
during the learning curve phase, without addressing the
long-term outcomes, as the postoperative interval at the
time of writing was insufficient for such an analysis. The
concept was primarily proposed to assess its feasibility in
graft harvesting within the study. In the future, the “LIMA
isthmus”-based graft harvesting approach will be evaluated
in a larger cohort of patients, with a focus on long-term
outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS
The OFCCJ could serve as a reliable anatomical landmark
to guide robotic LIMA harvesting. “LIMA isthmus”-based
standardized robotic LIMA harvesting could significantly
reduce the time to determine LIMA and the time for graft harvesting process, and minimize the risk of inadvertent bleeding
events. This could be particularly beneficial for novice surgeons during the initial learning curve phase of this
procedure.
