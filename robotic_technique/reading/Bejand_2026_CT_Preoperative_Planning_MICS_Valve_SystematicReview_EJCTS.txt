Abstract
Objectives: Minimally invasive cardiac surgery (MICS) offers significant advantages over conventional sternotomy,

including reduced trauma and faster recovery. Preoperative planning is crucial, with computed tomography (CT) emerg­
ing as an essential imaging tool. This systematic review aims to evaluate CT’s role in MICS planning, focusing on imaging
protocols, surgical guidance, and patient outcomes.

Methods: This study included studies published between January 2003 and January 2025 that examined the role of CT

Results: Initial search found 378 studies, and 17 studies were included in the review. The studies highlight CT’s value in

assessing surgical feasibility, vascular access, and risk stratification. CT aids in selecting arterial cannulation sites, guiding
aortic occlusion strategies, and optimizing port placement. It also identifies contraindications, such as extensive athero­
sclerosis and anatomical abnormalities, that may necessitate sternotomy.

Conclusions: Current evidence underscores CT’s critical role in optimizing MICS planning and enhancing patient safety.
Keywords: computed tomography; minimally invasive cardiac surgery; preoperative planning; valvular heart disease.

Introduction
Valvular heart disease (VHD) remains a leading cause of
cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide.1,2
Surgical intervention for VHD traditionally involved sternot­
omy approach. Currently, minimally invasive cardiac sur­
gery (MICS) is emerging as a transformative approach. By
using a right thoracotomy incision rather than a median
sternotomy, MICS minimizes surgical trauma, reduces re­
covery time, and improves postoperative outcomes.3 MICS
achieved high rates of valve repair with no significant differ­
ence with sternotomy approach and facilitate earlier return
to normal activities.4,5 Preoperative imaging plays a crucial
role in planning and optimizing these procedures, with
computed tomography (CT) emerging as an essential tool
for preoperative assessment and procedural planning.6
Preoperative CT assessment is valuable not only for minim­
ally invasive mitral procedures, but also for planning min­
imally invasive aortic valve surgery. It plays a key role in
selecting the most suitable access route among several
established approaches, including right anterior thoracot­
omy, upper or lower hemi-sternotomy, and axillary inci­
sions. The use of partial upper sternotomy for aortic valve
replacement resulted in preservation of chest wall integrity
and reduced blood loss and pain compared to standard
median sternotomy. For mitral valve surgery, the right
anterolateral minithoracotomy is currently the standard
minimally invasive approach, and the robot-assisted port
access has increasingly been adopted.3
The progressive evolution from conventional sternotomy,
fully endoscopic, and ultimately robotic valve surgery high­
lights the growing importance of preoperative CT imaging
in surgical planning. As the surgical access becomes in­
creasingly limited and dependent on precise anatomical
visualization, CT helps identify anatomical challenges,
ensuring safer procedures.7 A significant advantage of CT
imaging in MICS is its rapid acquisition of high-resolution
images, allowing for efficient preoperative planning.6
Advances in CT technology have also led to the develop­
ment of dose reduction strategies, minimizing radiation
exposure while maintaining diagnostic image quality.8

High-resolution CT allows accurate evaluation of access
routes, instrument trajectories, and potential anatomic
constraints, which are critical for ensuring both feasibility
and safety in less invasive and robotic-assisted operations.
However, there is a lack of standardized protocols, clear
indications, and consensus on image interpretation, high­
lighting the need for further research to optimize its clinic­
al application in MICS.
This systematic review aims to evaluate published evi­
dence on the role of CT in planning minimally invasive
valvular heart surgery, focusing on imaging protocols,
surgical guidance, risk assessment, and its impact on pro­
cedural outcomes.

Methods
Literature search strategy
This review included studies published between January
2003 and January 2025 that examined the role of CT imag­
ing in planning minimally invasive valvular heart surgery. A
comprehensive search was conducted in Medical Literature
Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), Cumulative
Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and
Excerpta Medica Database (EMBASE) databases using pre­
defined keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH)
terms related to CT imaging, angiography, minimally inva­
sive surgery, robotic surgery, aortic valve surgery, and mi­
tral valve surgery. The search strategy combined key
blocks—preoperative imaging, cardiac surgery, and valve
surgery—using Boolean operators to refine relevant stud­
ies while minimizing irrelevant results. Specific MeSH
terms and keywords were added to each block to increase
the chance of finding relevant studies. MeSH terms and
keywords searched in this research were: CT imaging,
Angiography, Minimally invasive surgery, Robotic surgery,
Aortic valve surgery, Mitral valve surgery. Last search date
was February 4, 2025. The reference lists of relevant journal
articles were reviewed to identify additional studies that
may have been missed in the primary database search.

Eligibility criteria

Data extraction and critical appraisal
Study selection was conducted by 2 independent reviewers
(B.S. and E.A.B.). Title and abstracts were screened according
to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by duplicate
assessment of full-text articles for eligibility. Discrepancies in
study selection were resolved through discussion, with arbi­
tration by a senior reviewer (S.M.) when consensus could
not be achieved.
Data extraction was performed independently by both
reviewers. Extracted variables included study design, popu­
lation characteristics, CT acquisition protocols, surgical ap­
proach, and outcomes relevant to preoperative planning,
intraoperative guidance, and postoperative complications.
All extracted data were cross-checked, and disagreements
were reconciled by consensus. Key findings were synthe­
sized into a narrative review that highlights the clinical
value of CT imaging in MICS.
Risk of bias in observational studies was evaluated in­
dependently by both reviewers using the NewcastleOttawa Scale. Case series and narrative reviews were
appraised qualitatively, given the absence of validated
standardized tools for these study types.

Figure sources
The figures presented in the main text and supplementary
material are original images acquired in our department.
All images were fully anonymized. Written informed con­
sent for their use in research/educational purposes was
obtained from all patients in accordance with our institu­
tional data governance policies.

Results
Quantity of evidence
A search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CINAHL identified 378
studies. Screening of duplicate studies removed 32 stud­
ies, resulting in 346 studies. Title and abstract screening

excluded 287 studies that were either unrelated to MICS
or focused on non-cardiac procedures. Full-text review of
the remaining 59 studies led to the exclusion of an add­
itional 42 studies. A total of 17 studies on MICS met the in­
clusion criteria for final analysis (Figure S1).

Quality of evidence
The 17 studies consisted of 5 observational studies,9-13
2 case series,14,15 and 10 reviews,6,7,16-23 representing a
diverse range of evidence levels.
Risk of bias for the observational studies were assessed
using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (Table S1). Three stud­
ies scored 3 out of 9, indicating relatively low methodo­
logical quality, predominantly due to limitations in
control selection and comparability domains. Two studies
scored 7 out of 9, reflecting high quality with better con­
trol selection. Retrospective designs and relatively small
sample sizes were common limitations across these ob­
servational studies. The key findings of the observational
studies were summarized in Table S2.
Standardized tools for quality assessment were not ap­
plicable to the case series and literature reviews. These
studies were assessed qualitatively by the co-authors,
which consisted of senior consultant radiologists and car­
diothoracic surgeons. However, they inherently carry a
higher risk of selection, reporting, and publication biases.

Observational studies involving human subjects under­
going heart surgery were included to assess the clinical
applications and outcomes of preoperative CT imaging.
Only peer-reviewed full-text articles were included, while
conference abstracts and studies on other imaging modal­
ities, such as MRI, were excluded. All types of full-text stud­
ies, such as case series or reviews, were included. Given
the limited volume of primary research on CT assessment
in MICS, this review incorporated both primary evidence
(observational studies and case series) and secondary
sources (narrative reviews) to achieve a comprehensive
synthesis of current knowledge. This methodological ap­
proach is consistent with PRISMA recommendations for
systematic reviews encompassing heterogeneous study
designs. Studies unrelated to MICS or focused on noncardiac procedures, including those on CT imaging with­
out clinical relevance to MICS planning, were excluded.

the femoral artery.16,14,18

The accuracy of coronary atherosclerosis assessment
on CT can be compromised by motion artefacts. To min­
imize these, pharmacological heart rate control is com­
monly used to lower both heart rate and heart rate
variability.26 When not contraindicated, heart rate reduc­
tion—administered either intravenously or orally—can be
considered to enhance the image quality of the coronary
CT angiogram.
Current evidence suggests preoperative CT angiography
to evaluate the aorta and peripheral arteries.9,16 Variability
in CT protocols directly influences the anatomical informa­
tion that can be obtained. Scan acquisition should be tail­
ored to the specific clinical questions after discussion with
the operating surgeon. At minimum, an ECG-gated angio­
graphic study of the thoracic cavity is required to evaluate
the cardiac structures, thoracic aorta, and the chest wall.
The field of view can be extended when additional

information is needed—for instance, to assess the carotid
arteries or peripheral vessels for cannulation suitability.
Radiation exposure and contrast volume are influenced by
several factors, including the extent of anatomical cover­
age, patient body size, and the imaging technique used.
Assessment of the venous system may require adjust­
ments to the CT imaging protocol, including modifications
to contrast volume, image acquisition timing, and radi­
ation exposure settings.

Limitations of this review and
current evidence
The studies included in this review are predominantly retro­
spective, lacking high-level evidence from randomized con­
trolled trials. The evidence in this review was derived
primarily from descriptive studies, narrative reviews, and
small observational case series. While these sources pro­
vide important qualitative insights into procedural planning
and anatomical evaluation, but limited quantitative data for
direct clinical decision-making. Thus, the current evidence
should be regarded as descriptive rather than definitive.
While observational data suggest improved patient selec­
tion and perioperative outcomes with preoperative CT, ro­
bust prospective trials are needed to quantify these
benefits conclusively.
Another challenge is the absence of standardized CT
imaging protocols across institutions. Variability in acqui­
sition techniques, contrast administration, and image in­
terpretation may lead to inconsistent decision-making
and affect the reproducibility of findings. Moreover, con­
cerns regarding radiation exposure, particularly in
younger patients, highlight the need for optimized lowdose imaging protocols.
Additionally, while CT is invaluable in surgical planning,
it is not infallible. Certain intraoperative challenges, such
as unexpected adhesions or dynamic haemodynamic
changes, may not be fully predicted by preoperative imag­
ing alone. Therefore, multimodal imaging approaches re­
main essential for comprehensive patient assessment.

Future directions
Future research should focus on standardizing imaging
protocols and conducting prospective trials comparing
CT-guided MICS planning with traditional approaches.
Further studies evaluating cost-effectiveness, radiation

dose optimization, and integration of advanced imaging
techniques, such as artificial intelligence-assisted ana­
lysis, could refine CT’s clinical application.

Conclusion
While current evidence supports the utility of CT in MICS,
its role should be further validated through high-quality
trials. Nonetheless, CT remains a powerful tool that may
enhance surgical precision, improve patient selection,
and minimize complications, reinforcing its importance in
modern cardiac surgery.
