Methods: We retrospectively analysed patients who underwent LAA closure using a continuous polypropylene suture technique during
mitral valve surgery with the da Vinci system. Closure completeness was assessed via electrocardiogram-gated computed tomography
(CT), and 3D imaging was used to localize incomplete sites.
Results: Of 125 patients, 100 underwent postoperative CT. Complete closure (cLAA) was achieved in 76%, while 24% had incomplete clo­
sure (iLAA), typically at the mitral valve junction or suture start. These showed a characteristic “speech bubble” appearance. iLAA was as­
sociated with tricuspid valve repair and low V1 f-wave amplitude. Stroke rates were similar between groups over 24 months.
Conclusions: Incomplete LAA closure occurred in 24% of cases despite direct visualization using robotics. Anatomical factors likely con­
tribute more than technique alone. For high-risk patients with long-standing AF, techniques such as lumen crushing or external clips may
enhance closure success. Further refinements are needed to improve the reliability of internal LAA closure methods.

INTRODUCTION
Atrial fibrillation (AF) significantly increases the risk of intracar­
diac thromboembolism, with the left atrial appendage (LAA) be­
ing the source of clots in up to 90% of non-rheumatic AF
cases.1,2 Current guidelines recommend LAA closure in AF
patients undergoing mitral valve or maze surgery.3 The LAAOS
study confirmed its safety and efficacy in reducing cardiogenic
stroke.4 Robotic surgery, particularly with the da Vinci system,
has improved precision and visualization in minimally invasive
procedures. During mitral valve surgery, the system allows for si­
multaneous LAA closure from within the left atrium (LA), en­
hancing efficiency. Prior studies have reported high rates of
incomplete closure with suture ligation, leading to increased
thromboembolic risk,5–8 though these were based on direct vi­
sion approaches. No studies have focused specifically on robotic
LAA closure. Enhanced visualization with robotics may improve
closure success through precise suturing. Although some studies
addressed lumen closure,9,10 few analyse anatomical sites prone
to reopening.
This study investigates anatomical sites of LAA reopening us­
ing ECG-gated CT, based on 78 months of robotic LAA closure
experience since July 2018. It aims to identify technical principles
that improve outcomes with the double continuous-suture
technique.

METHODS
This retrospective study was approved by the institutional ethics
committee (TGE02656-025), with informed consent waived. LAA
closure was performed during mitral valve surgery using the da
Vinci system in patients with paroxysmal or persistent AF. This
study did not involve the collection or long-term storage of bio­
logical materials or data for future unspecified use; therefore,
the WMA Declaration of Taipei was not applicable. All proce­
dures were conducted by the same surgeon (Y.N.) and assistant
(M.K.). Closure outcomes were assessed by ECG-gated car­
diac CT.
Potential causes of incomplete closure were examined from
patient, anatomical, and technical perspectives. We also evalu­
ated whether these factors contributed to embolic or bleeding
complications. Follow-up was via outpatient visits or phone
interviews at 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months postoperatively, then
annually. These methods helped minimize verification bias.

Stroke was defined as a neurological deficit lasting over
24 hours, confirmed by neurologist and imaging where possible.
Postoperative stroke required both clinical and CT or MRI con­
firmation. Major bleeding was defined per ISTH criteria11: fatal
bleeding, critical organ bleeding (eg, intracranial, pericardial), or
bleeding causing ≥2.0 g/dl haemoglobin drop or ≥2-unit
transfusion.

Surgical technique
Under general anaesthesia and one-lung ventilation in a halfsupine position, a 4-cm incision between the right fourth and
fifth ribs served as the working port. Femoral arterio-venous
cannulation established cardiopulmonary bypass as previously
described.12 Ports were inserted, the pericardium opened and
retracted. A cardioplegia cannula was inserted into the ascend­
ing aorta and clamped via the transverse sinus. The robot was
docked and thoracoscopic procedures performed. Myocardial
arrest was induced per standard protocol.13 The LA was incised
using robotic scissors, and the mitral valve exposed with
a retractor.
LAA closure used a continuous double polypropylene suture,
starting ventrally. From the 65th case, felt-reinforced nodal
sutures were added; from the 83rd case, horizontal mattress
sutures were combined with over-and-over sutures. Extra nodal
sutures were placed as needed.

Computed tomography
Preoperative ECG-gated CT assessed LA and LAA volume, size,
morphology, and proximity to coronary arteries. Measurements
were taken using Philips IntelliSpace Portal (slice thickness
0.8 mm, increment 0.4 mm, and 75% cardiac phase). LAA mor­
phology was classified as cactus, chicken wing, windsock, or
cauliflower.14
Three to 6 months postoperatively, contrast-enhanced CT was
performed unless contraindicated. Early and delayed phases
were analysed using ECG synchronization. Incomplete closure
(iLAA) was identified by delayed-phase contrast in the LAA lu­
men. Complete closure (cLAA) showed no residual contrast. For
iLAA, 3D LA images were reconstructed to locate communica­
tion sites relative to the mitral valve.

Statistical analysis
Basic statistics were calculated for baseline demographic and
clinical parameters. Continuous variables are presented as
medians with interquartile ranges, expressed as the 25th and
75th percentiles (p25; p75). Categorical variables are
expressed as frequencies and percentages (%). Time-to-event
data, such as stroke incidence, were analysed using KaplanMeier survival curves. To explore factors associated with iLAA,
univariate logistic regression analysis was performed. Variables
deemed clinically relevant or demonstrating potential associa­
tions were subsequently included in a multivariate logistic re­
gression model to identify independent predictors. A 2-sided
P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. All sta­
tistical analyses were performed using Excel statistical software
(Ekuseru-Toukei; Social Survey Research Information Co.,
Tokyo, Japan).

RESULTS
Between July 2018 and December 2023, a total of 125 patients
with paroxysmal or persistent AF associated with mitral valve
disease underwent LAA closure during mitral valve surgery using
the da Vinci surgical system. No adverse events, including bleed­
ing, arrhythmia, myocardial ischaemia, cardiac or noncardiac in­
jury, stroke, or death, were reported. Notably, no left circumflex
artery injury occurred during LAA closure.
Among these patients, 100 were eligible for postoperative
evaluation using electrocardiogram-synchronized computed
tomography. The 25 excluded patients included 12 with renal
dysfunction and 7 with contraindications to contrast media,
such as asthma or a contrast media allergy. Additionally, 6
patients were ineligible for CT imaging due to difficulties visit­
ing the hospital as outpatients, such as living too far away.
Patient demographic data are summarized in Table 1. The
median age was 73 years, 55% were female, and the median
body surface area was 1.51 m2 . Twelve patients had chronic
renal failure, though none required dialysis. Paroxysmal AF
was present in 40%, while 60% had long-standing persistent
AF. The CHADS2 and CHA2DS2-VASc scores were 2 and 4, re­
spectively. Cardiac function was generally preserved, with an
median left ventricular ejection fraction of 67.2% and a left

atrial diameter of 47.6 mm. Most patients had moderate to se­
vere mitral regurgitation, and 51% had moderate to severe tri­
cuspid regurgitation.
Postoperative computed tomography angiography in the
evaluated patients revealed complete LAA closure in 76 (76%)
cases and incomplete closure in 24 (24%) cases. In incomplete
closure cases, a small, speech balloon-like opening was observed
on imaging (Figure 1).
Surgery-related factors are summarized in Table 2. Among
patients with incomplete closure, tricuspid valve plasty was per­
formed in several cases, whereas maze procedures were less
commonly documented. There were no early deaths, postopera­
tive haemorrhages requiring reoperation, or in-hospital strokes.
During follow-up, residual or recurrent AF occurred in 35% of all
patients. This was observed in 14 patients (58%) within the iLAA
group. Postoperative medications, including DOAC and VKA,
were comparable across groups. The follow-up period exceeded
20 months in both groups.

Postoperative CT evaluations
The median time from surgery to CT evaluation was 4 months
(interquartile range, 3-13 months). A CT scan from the cLAA
group (Figure 2) showed complete LAA closure, with reduced
volume and a smooth, spherical LA. EP planning images revealed
a regular LAA lumen and a clean intima (refer to Video 1 for
procedural details).
In contrast, CT scans from the iLAA group (Figure 3-1 and
3-2) revealed blood flow entering the LAA through a small pore,
with unchanged LAA volume. EP planning images identified
openings, most commonly at the ceiling side where closure
begins (50%, Figure 3-1) and at the left inflection point near the
mitral valve and left superior pulmonary vein (45%, Figure 3-2).
Videos 2 and 3 provide procedural details. In one iLAA case, the
LAA was fully reopened by cutting the suture, while in 3 cases,
the openings were challenging to locate (Table 1, Figure 4).
Twenty-four iLAA patients underwent additional follow-up
CT. The second follow-ups, at an mean of 16.8 ± 10 months (18
patients), and third follow-ups, at 26.6 ± 4.7 months (4 patients),
showed that only one patient achieved complete LAA closure by
18 months. In the remaining 23 patients, persistent blood flow
indicated incomplete closure throughout the follow-up period.

Postoperative late events
The follow-up period ranged from 3 to 66 months, with a me­
dian of 24 months (IQR: 15-38 months). Median follow-up was
31 months in the cLAA group and 21.5 months in the iLAA
group. This difference is presented descriptively, as the study
was not powered to assess statistical differences in follow-up
duration. Anticoagulant therapy (DOAC or VKA) was discon­
tinued in 52% of patients based on AF status and patient con­
sultation. In the cLAA group, 30 patients continued DOAC
therapy, and 5 remained on VKA. Among the 24 iLAA cases, 10
patients achieved sinus rhythm, and despite incomplete clo­
sure, the embolic risk was deemed low due to the speech
bubble-like morphology. After thorough explanation, anticoa­
gulation was discontinued. The remaining 14 patients had
persistent AF and were generally indicated for anticoagulation;

For the first 3 postoperative months, all patients received direct
oral anticoagulants (DOAC) or warfarin (vitamin K antagonist;
VKA) regardless of rhythm. VKAs were used after valve replace­
ment, and DOACs after valve repair. If sinus rhythm was con­
firmed on ECG at 3 months, anticoagulation was discontinued.
Contrast-enhanced CT was performed between 3 and 6 months
to assess left atrial flow; however, the presence or absence of re­
sidual LAA flow was not a determining factor for continuing
anticoagulation. Thereafter, therapy was individualized, except in
patients with cardiac dysfunction or valve replacement, where it
was routinely continued. In patients with high bleeding risk (eg,
prior GI bleeding), anticoagulation was discontinued even with
persistent AF.

DISCUSSION
The LAAOS III trial4 demonstrated that LAA closure performed
during cardiac surgery effectively prevents stroke; however, it
did not explicitly discuss the closure methods or the quality of
closure. Notably, the trial reported that double-layer linear clo­
sure from the endocardial side was utilized in 13.8% of patients,
highlighting the importance of reliable intraoperative assessment
through transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE). However,
electrocardiogram-gated cardiac CT was not employed in that
study to evaluate closure integrity. Our study is the first to sys­
tematically assess the indications and limitations of LAA closure
using the da Vinci surgical system, and specifically to employ
electrocardiogram-gated cardiac CT imaging for postoperative
evaluation of closure effectiveness.

Aryana et al15 analysed 72 patients who underwent simulta­
neous mitral valve and maze surgery with suture closure of the
LAA from the cardiac lumen, using CT for evaluation. They
found cLAA in 64% and iLAA in 24%, similar to this study. SSE
risk correlated with the degree of LAA closure. At 44 ± 19
months post-surgery, recurrent AF rates were similar, but SSE
occurred in 2% of cLAA and 24% of iLAA patients. Notably, all
patients with SSE had recurrent AF and were not on DOAC. The
annualized SSE risk was 1.9% overall, 6.5% for iLAA patients, and
14.4% for iLAA patients not on DOAC. Univariable and multivar­
iable analyses identified iLAA as the sole independent SSE pre­
dictor, highlighting the need for high-quality LAA closure.
Branzoli et al16 studied patients undergoing LAA closure before
robotic mitral valve repair, similar to this study. Using a 2-layer
running 4-0 polytetrafluoroethylene suture, they evaluated
results with transoesophageal echocardiography, achieving suc­
cessful ligation in 87.7% of cases. Of these, 9.6% had small jet
flow into the LAA but no residual stumps, while 2.7% had unde­
termined flow. In our cohort, intraoperative TEE revealed unde­
termined LAA flow in some patients, but postoperative TEE was
not performed. In some cases, contrast-enhanced CT detected
blood flow even when TEE did not, warranting further
investigation.

There are 2 primary approaches to LAA closure: the medial
approach, as used in the present cases, and the lateral approach.
Traditional external methods involved excisional suture closure
and stapling,8 but recent outcomes with epicardial clips have
been favourable. Younju et al17 analysed 108 AF patients who
underwent epicardial LAA clipping with or without simultaneous
cardiac surgery using a minimally invasive approach. They
reported a procedural success rate of 92.2%, with postoperative
CT confirming complete LAA elimination without residual
stumps or device-related complications such as bleeding, migra­
tion, or reoperation. The lateral approach is generally considered
more reliable for securely closing the LAA from its base. Antaki
et al18 also reported success with a lateral approach using the
DaVinci system, achieving effective closure with a clip. However,
this approach is technically demanding and adds complexity
when combined with right-sided mitral valve surgery, requiring
additional ports. Its ability to consistently identify the LAA’s ori­
gin compared to a left-sided approach remains unclear. Kuzmin
et al19 highlighted a risk with clip placement: if the clip is too
close to the LAA base, it may obstruct or damage the LCx.
Preoperative CT is crucial to assess the spatial relationship be­
tween the LAA and LCx. Despite their advantages, the high cost
of clips remains a significant concern.
Increased thromboembolic risk associated with incomplete
LAA closure after surgical ligation has been reported,5 but defini­
tions vary across studies. These include “residual anatomic struc­
ture between the mitral valve base and the left superior
pulmonary vein,”5,6 “residual stump of 1.0 cm or more,” and
“persistent flow in the LAA after surgical exclusion.”7 In line with
Healey et al, we define incomplete LAA closure as persistent
flow into the LAA. Anatomical factors significantly contribute to

incomplete LAA closure. The LAA’s unique embryonic origin and
trabeculated walls, resembling ventricles due to pectinate
muscles,20,21 contrast with the smooth-walled atrium. Its com­
plex anatomy, located in the left atrioventricular sulcus near the
left circumflex coronary artery and great cardiac vein,22,23
impacts closure success. An inflection point along the LAA clo­
sure line often becomes prone to recanalization, especially un­
der resumed blood flow after cardiac arrest. Increased left atrial
blood volume also raises the likelihood of reopening. Closing
only the LAA entrance may fail due to its stretched anatomy. In
our study, incomplete closures showed “speech bubble” forma­
tions, likely due to this mechanism. Furthermore, the risk of in­
complete closure tended to be higher in patients with AF who
exhibited low-voltage f-waves, cases in which completion of the
maze procedure was not feasible, and cases requiring concomi­
tant tricuspid valve surgery. Rather than being attributable to
surgical technique, these findings likely reflect advanced myo­
cardial remodelling in AF, which leads to anatomical changes
that predispose the LAA to form residual spaces or pouches after
closure. This interpretation is supported by our multivariate
analysis (Table S1), which identified tricuspid valve annuloplasty
and low-voltage f-waves in lead V1 as independent predictors of
incomplete LAA closure. These variables likely serve as surrogate

markers of atrial remodelling, rather than direct mechanical con­
tributors to closure failure. To address these anatomical chal­
lenges, we are exploring a technique that involves collapsing
and sealing the LAA lumen rather than simply suturing its en­
trance. This approach is particularly crucial for achieving com­
plete closure in high-risk patients. We have applied this lumen
collapse technique in 10 cases, successfully closing the LAA with­
out creating pouch-like formations. However, this method car­
ries an increased risk of bleeding, necessitating caution,
especially in cases with strong adhesions.
It goes without saying that the primary goal of closing the LAA
is to prevent cardiogenic stroke. However, another major pur­
pose may be to discontinue anticoagulant medication to address
the risk of bleeding complications. While there is no clear evi­
dence that closing the LAA leads to discontinuation of anticoag­
ulant therapy, it has been reported that more than 90% of
thrombogenic sites in AF cases occur in the LAA,24 and thus, it
may help to stop anticoagulation in those with a bleeding risk. A
report by Antaki et al18 noted that nearly all patients discontin­
ued DOAC after LAA closure with use of a clip, except for one
with a CHA2DS2-VASc score of 9. Additionally, post-clip bleed­
ing was noted in only 2 of 108 gastrointestinal haemorrhage
patients, while no transient ischaemic attack or stroke occur­
rence was noted in the 25 patients who completed the 12month follow-up examination. That report noted that lack of
significant haemorrhage or stroke after LAA closure with use of a
clip lends credibility to the concept of epicardial clip exclusion
for the LAA. As for the present cases, anticoagulants could be
discontinued in 52 patients and the only bleeding complication
noted was a spinal subdural Haemorrhage in one in whom
DOAC was continued. There are no clear criteria for discontinu­
ing anticoagulant administration. Should the decision be based
on maintenance of sinus rhythm, complete closure of the LAA,
or both? Further accumulation of evidence will be needed to ad­
dress these issues.

Study limitations
This retrospective, single-centre study has several limitations, in­
cluding selection bias and lack of a control group. CT imaging
was not performed in patients with renal dysfunction or contrast
allergy, which may limit generalizability. Image quality may have
been affected by suboptimal heart rate control. Additionally,
outcomes reflect procedures by a single surgical team,
