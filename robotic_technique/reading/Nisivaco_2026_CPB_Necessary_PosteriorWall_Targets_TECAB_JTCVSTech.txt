Dark sides and warm rides: Is cardiopulmonary bypass
necessary for grafting posterior wall targets in robotic
totally endoscopic coronary bypass?
Sarah Nisivaco, MD, Hiroto Kitahara, MD, Sandeep Nathan, MD, and Husam H. Balkhy, MD
ABSTRACT
Objective: Robotic off-pump totally-endoscopic coronary artery bypass (TECAB) is
the least-invasive form of surgical coronary revascularization. It allows for sternalsparing, multivessel, total-arterial revascularization. Although most published series
focus on anterior-wall targets, little is known about the feasibility and outcomes of
posterior wall grafting. We sought to evaluate this in a robotic beating-heart TECAB
series with grafting of posterior-wall, “dark side,” targets.
Methods: We retrospectively reviewed 137 (of a total 983) patients undergoing
robotic beating-heart TECAB between July 2013 and August 2025 who underwent
grafting to at least 1 posterior-wall target—defined as distal obtuse marginal (OM2/
OM3), posterior descending, or posterolateral branch (of the RCA). Peripheral CPB
was instituted selectively for hemodynamic intolerance.
Results: Mean age was 66 ± 9.8 years; 16.8% were female; and ejection
fraction ≤40% occurred in 15.3%. Multivessel TECAB was performed in 102 patients
(74.5%); bilateral internal thoracic arteries were used in 98 of 102 (96.1%). Cardiopulmonary bypass was required in 5 patients (3.6%); there were no conversions to
sternotomy. Operative time was 317 ± 72 minutes. Early outcomes included atrial
fibrillation 10%, acute kidney injury 1.5%, stroke 0.7%, and in-hospital mortality
0.7%; hospital stay was 2.2 ± 0.8 days; and 97% were discharged home. Follow-up
was 100% complete (47 ± 34 months); overall mortality was 15%, cardiac mortality
2.9%, myocardial infarction 2.2%, and repeat revascularization 5.1% (all unplanned
percutaneous coronary intervention). Freedom from major adverse cardiac and
cerebrovascular events was 92%.
Conclusions: In an experienced program, posterior-wall targets are not a contraindication to robotic beating-heart TECAB. These grafts can be performed safely
with infrequent need for bypass, excellent early recovery, and durable midterm
outcomes, expanding TECAB beyond anterior-wall–only revascularization. (JTCVS

Posterior wall grafting in robotic endoscopic
CABG has traditionally been considered not
feasible due to limited exposure and hemodynamic concerns. In an experienced robotic
beating-heart TECAB program using a robotic

reached. This study demonstrates low need for
CPB and favorable outcomes, thus expanding
the applicability of robotic coronary surgery.

Despite these advantages, one of the perceived limitations
of robotic off-pump TECAB has been the ability to achieve
multivessel grafting. Most published series of robotic
CABG have focused on anterior wall targets, whereas grafting
to the distal circumflex or right coronary artery (RCA)
branches is rare. The technical challenge of exposing and
stabilizing these posterior wall vessels, combined with concerns about hemodynamic tolerance during exposure, has
led to the notion that these targets were not feasible to graft
using a robotic totally endoscopic approach off-pump.
We have had extensive experience with robotic multivessel
beating-heart TECAB, including the routine use of multiarterial grafting with bilateral internal thoracic artery (BITA) use
with the aid of the EndoWrist stabilizer on the Da Vinci Si
(third-generation) robot (Intuitive Surgical).9 Within this practice, we have developed the experience of grafting posterior
wall targets—what we term the “dark side” of the heart—in
an endoscopic setting using a beating-heart approach with
standard off-pump coronary bypass principles.
In this study, we sought to evaluate outcomes in patients
undergoing robotic beating-heart TECAB with grafting of at
least 1 “dark-side” posterior wall target with a left or right
internal thoracic artery (LITA or RITA) conduit. We hypothesized that with adequate stabilization, posterior wall grafting
can be safely performed on the beating-heart with infrequent
need for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) support—a “warm

METHODS
Ethical Statement
This deidentified study was approved by our institutional
review board (#18-0742, April 29, 2025). Individual patient
consent was waived.
Data Availability Statement
The data underlying this article are available in the article

Study Design and Patient Population
We performed a retrospective review of all patients undergoing robotic beating-heart TECAB at our institution between
July 2013 and August 2025. A total of 983 consecutive cases
were identified from a prospectively maintained database. Of
these, 137 patients underwent grafting to at least 1 posterior
wall coronary target and formed the study cohort for this
report. Posterior wall targets were defined as distal obtuse
marginal branches (obtuse marginal branch 2 or 3), posterior
descending artery, or posterolateral branches of the right
coronary artery. Conduit for bypass was either the LITA or
RITA as part of a single or multivessel grafting strategy.
Baseline demographics, perioperative data, and follow-up
outcomes were collected.

posterior wall (“dark-side”) branches, the decision for
revascularization is made on the basis of discussions with
the Heart Team and referring cardiologists to ensure both complete revascularization and a treatment course tailored to each
individual patient. Often, small nondominant “dark-side”
branches are selected for percutaneous coronary intervention
(PCI) as part of a hybrid revascularization strategy. In cases
of large or dominant dark-side branches that feed a large
territory of myocardium, these may be felt to be better served
with an arterial graft as discussed by the Heart Team.
The majority of procedures were performed through
standard left-sided port placement using the da Vinci Si
robotic system (Intuitive Surgical) and the EndoWrist stabilizer system—a technique we have previously described.6,9,10
In cases of isolated RCA disease (n = 26 patients) mirrorimage right sided ports were placed. ITA conduits were
harvested in a skeletonized fashion. All anastomoses were performed on the beating-heart (with use of an intracoronary
shunt) via a robotic-assisted running sutured technique (or
an anastomotic device—the C-Port Flex A distal anastomotic
stapler [Aesculap]—in the first half of the cohort). Peripheral
CPB was instituted in cases of significant hemodynamic
intolerance during target exposure, with the most common
criteria prompting CPB being hypotension or ventricular
arrhythmias.
Our sequence of grafting strategy follows standard
off-pump techniques. The easiest targets with the least
potential for hemodynamic insult and the largest revascularized territory are grafted first. This usually means the LAD
is grafted first, followed by posterior wall targets.

RESULTS
Preoperative Characteristics
Preoperative characteristics are included in Table 1. The
mean age was 66 ± 9.8 years; 23 patients (16.8%) were
female. In total, 47 (34%) had diabetes, including
18 (13%) who were insulin-dependent. Preoperative atrial
fibrillation (AF) was present in 13 patients (9.5%), previous
stroke in 10 (7.3%), and previous MI in 38 (28%). Mean
body mass index was 29.2 ± 4.1 kg/m2 (42% with body
mass index >30). Mean ejection fraction (EF) was 53 ±
10%, with 21 patients (15%) having EF <40%. Six
patients (4.4%) had undergone previous cardiac surgery,
all which involved CABG. Mean Society of Thoracic Surgeons predicted risk of mortality score was 1.2 ± 1.9%.

Follow-Up
Clinical follow-up was obtained annually through direct patient contact or electronic medical record review. Outcomes
assessed included all-cause mortality, cardiac-related mortality, major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events
(MACCE), and repeat revascularization including unplanned
PCI or any repeat cardiac surgery. MACCE is defined as
myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, cardiovascular mortality,
and any repeat revascularization. MI was classified per Fourth
Universal Definition; stroke per symptoms with confirmed imaging; AKI per KDIGO (Kidney Disease Improving Global
Outcomes) guidelines. Early graft patency was assessed in
hybrid revascularization patients who underwent PCI after
TECAB.

Early Postoperative Outcomes
Early postoperative outcomes are shown in Table 4. Postoperative AF occurred in 14 patients (10%), AKI in 2 patients
(1.5%), and 1 patient (0.7%) had a postoperative stroke.
There were no postoperative MIs or take-backs for bleeding.
The mean intensive care unit length of stay (LOS) was
1.1±0.3 days, and mean hospital stay was 2.2±0.8 days.
One 30-day mortality occurred (0.7%), with an observed/
expected of 0.61 using preoperative Society of Thoracic
Surgeons predicted risk of mortality score. 97% were discharged directly home; 30-day readmission occurred in 5 patients (3.6%).
For comparison, in the whole TECAB cohort the mean age
was 66.2 ± 10 years and mean EF 53 ± 12%. The average
number of grafts per patient was 1.62 ± 0.64. Multivessel
grafting occurred in 54% with 87% BITA use in this group.
Mean hospital LOS was 2.2 ± 0.87 days. Thirty-day mortality
was 0.69%.

DISCUSSION
In this study, we report outcomes of 137 patients who underwent robotic beating-heart TECAB with grafting to at least
one “dark-side” posterior wall target. To our knowledge, this
represents the largest reported series of grafting distal
posterior wall targets with this surgical technique. In this
group, CPB was required in only 3.6% of cases, and no
conversions to sternotomy were necessary. Our findings
demonstrate that these targets can be safely and effectively
addressed with a sternal-sparing robotic endoscopic
off-pump approach in experienced hands, provided an
adequate stabilization solution is available. Perioperative
and midterm outcomes were consistent with our general
TECAB experience. Although grafting these posterior vessels
relies on a high degree of experience with the robotic endoscopic approach, the ability to do so expands the reach of

Posterior wall vessels have traditionally been viewed as
unsuitable for grafting using an endoscopic beating heart
approach. Their location on the posterior wall, perceived
limited exposure via a port-only approach, and the potential
for hemodynamic compromise during stabilization has led
to this view. For this reason, most robotic CABG reports
are focused on LAD or other anterior wall targets only,2,11
and similarly for large minimally invasive direct coronary
artery bypass series in the literature.12,13
In our overall TECAB cohort (n = 983), multivessel
grafting was performed in 54% of the whole TECAB
group, with 87% BITA usage. The findings in this study
are an extension of our previous publications demonstrating
the feasibility and success of multivessel robotic TECAB
with BITA conduits.9,14 Multivessel disease, including

artery disease. A frequently used approach for these patients
in our practice is a hybrid or “advanced” hybrid strategy
(advanced describing a hybrid PCI with more than 1 internal thoracic artery graft) in order to achieve complete
revascularization.15,16 Our most common hybrid strategy
is to perform PCI after TECAB, primarily when the LAD
or other dominant coronary target is the primary diseased
vessel, allowing this to be surgically addressed first.
Occasionally we prefer a “reverse hybrid” strategy, in which
the PCI is performed before TECAB.17 One example of this
approach is in ACS, in which the culprit vessel is revascularized by PCI first. Another example in which we prefer
a reverse hybrid approach is when the feasibility of PCI is
not straightforward, allowing for subsequent adjustment
of the surgical plan if necessary.
Because we are a referral center for robotic coronary
surgery, we evaluate many patients with multivessel coronary
artery disease and discuss them within our weekly multidisciplinary coronary revascularization meeting. We attempt to
tailor the therapy not only to the individual patient but also
to the individual diseased vessel, particularly in patients with
multivessel disease. Careful scrutiny of each vessel and the
overall disease pattern, as well as a close working partnership
with interventional cardiologists, allows us to ensure that both
the sternal-sparing nature of the procedure and complete or
near complete revascularization are achieved. In this cohort,
the majority of patients were believed to be better served
with an arterial graft to their large, dominant circumflex or
RCA diseased branches and therefore underwent posterior
wall ITA grafting (as opposed to hybrid PCI). In some patients
however, and as mentioned above, PCI was felt not to be technically feasible, and thus we considered a robotic beating-heart
grafting approach for these vessels.
Early-term and midterm outcomes in this cohort were
favorable. Rates of AF, stroke, and renal dysfunction were
low, and nearly all patients were discharged directly to
home with a mean hospital LOS of 2.2 days. Midterm
follow-up was complete for all patients, with overall
survival of 85%, cardiac survival of 97%, and freedom
from MACCE of 92% at a mean of almost 4 years. These
results are comparable with those reported in our larger
series of robotic multivessel TECAB6 and are consistent
with contemporary traditional CABG results, including
those reported in large national datasets.18,19
We view avoiding CPB as an important aspect of this
approach. In our series, CPB was used only for the rare case
of hemodynamic instability during target exposure, allowing
the majority of patients to undergo an entirely off-pump procedure. This strategy not only avoids bypass-related morbidity
but also preserves the advantages of a “warm” beating-heart
technique including uninterrupted coronary perfusion when
CPB support is required. For the small subset of patients

It is important to note that the cornerstone of our surgical
approach is use of the robotic EndoWrist stabilizer, which is
inserted through a fourth robotic port placed in the left subcostal area and controlled by the surgeon at the console.20 In 137
patients receiving TECAB who underwent grafting to “darkside” targets of the heart in this study, CPB was required in
only 3.6%, and there were no conversions to sternotomy.
This experience suggests that, with appropriate exposure techniques and the stabilization afforded by the robotic platform,
posterior wall grafting can be achieved safely on the
beating-heart. Of note, anastomoses were primarily performed
with a robotic running-sutured technique; however, an anastomotic stapler device was used in the early stages. We have
previously compared our experience with these anastomotic
methods with no difference in outcomes found, aside from
operative time (shorter with the anastomotic device).21
The importance of the EndoWrist stabilizer and its versatility cannot be overstated in any robotic beating-heart cases,
but it has proven critical in the exposure of posterior wall targets as described here. Three important technical points
should be mentioned. The first is the position of the subcostal
stabilizer port relative to the midclavicular line. Although it is
routinely placed medial to the midclavicular line in cases of
anterior wall grafting, it should be positioned more laterally
when the posterior wall is intended for grafting in order to
avoid compressing the left ventricular apex which can lead

to hypotension. Second, as opposed to visualizing posterior
targets in traditional OPCAB (which requires significant
manipulation and elevation of the heart), exposing the posterior wall during TECAB requires only gentle manipulation
upwards with the stabilizer—because of to the lateral position
of the robotic ports and thus lateral visualization. Finally, in
cases of TECAB for isolated RCA disease using right-sided
ports, the stabilizer should be brought in via a port placed
as medial as possible to provide reach to the distal RCA or
posterior descending artery.22 The absence of the EndoWrist
stabilizer from newer robotic platforms, namely the fourthgeneration Da Vinci Xi, has forced us to continue performing
TECAB with the Si (third-generation) system. The recent
announcement from the DaVinci manufacturer of their intention to support cardiac surgery with an EndoWrist stabilizer
on the fifth-generation system, coupled with the introduction
of other robotic platforms to the market, is encouraging for the
continued progress and widespread adoption of these
procedures.
This study has limitations. It represents a retrospective,
single-center experience performed by a surgeon and team
with extensive robotic experience, with the surgeon well
past the robotic cardiac surgery learning curve. The applicability of these findings to lower-volume programs may be
limited. Routine angiographic surveillance was not performed
so graft patency data was limited to only those undergoing
hybrid revascularization after TECAB. Finally, the relatively
small sample size limits the ability to draw larger conclusions
regarding outcomes.

Despite these limitations, our data add to the growing
body of evidence that robotic beating-heart TECAB can
be safely extended to targets beyond the anterior wall. As
robotic platforms evolve with improved reach, visualization, and instrument dexterity, the technical barriers to these
grafts may be further reduced. Broader dissemination of
these techniques will also depend on structured training,
team familiarity, and institutional support.
CONCLUSIONS
In experienced centers, posterior wall targets are not a
contraindication to robotic beating-heart TECAB. Our
experience demonstrated that grafting these vessels can be
performed safely by an experienced team using adequate
epicardial stabilization with low need for CPB, no conversion
to sternotomy, and favorable early/midterm outcomes. This
capability expands the scope of robotic beating-heart TECAB
beyond just single-vessel grafting and supports its role as a
comprehensive revascularization strategy in appropriately
selected patients.
