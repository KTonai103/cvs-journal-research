Is concomitant tricuspid valve repair in patients
undergoing robotic mitral valve repair safe and effective?

ABSTRACT
Objectives: Robotic-assisted mitral valve repair (MVr) is a well-established procedure for management of degenerative mitral valve disease. Limited data regarding
concomitant robotic-assisted tricuspid valve repair (TVr) is available. This review
investigates prevalence and outcomes of concomitant robotic-assisted mitral and
tricuspid valve repair.
Methods: From 2014 to 2022, 839 patients underwent robotic-assisted MVr,
including 76 patients with moderate or greater tricuspid regurgitation and/or
tricuspid annular dilatation 40 mm. Among the 76 patients, 19 (25%) underwent
isolated MVr and 57 (75%) had concomitant mitral and tricuspid valve repair.
Outcome data between the 2 groups were analyzed.

open mitral surgery suggest that functional TR may
improve or resolve after the diseased MV is surgically corrected, subsequent data have shown late development of
significant TR and right heart failure in a subset of patients.3,4 Additionally, reoperative tricuspid surgery for
late TR has been shown to compromise long-term outcomes
after MVr and is associated with increased early and
midterm morbidity and mortality.5,6 Current consensus

Statistical Analysis
Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the ShapiroWilk test. Those with normal distribution are expressed as mean  SD
and those without as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical
and sequential variables are expressed as the number and percentage of
patients. Wilcoxon signed-rank test with continuity correction and
Friedman rank-sum test were applied to examine both groups’ serial
echocardiography results over the follow-up period. The statistical analysis utilized BlueSky Statistics software version 10.3 (BlueSky Statistics
LLC).

MVr and TVr versus isolated robotic-assisted MVr for patients with degenerative MR and known functional TR.
We found that short-term clinical outcomes between the
2 groups were similar with acceptable low morbidity, excellent survival, and early freedom from reoperation when
performing concomitant TVr during planned robotic MVr
among patients with moderate or greater TR and/or significant tricuspid annular dilatation (40 mm).
Functional regurgitation of the TV commonly occurs as a
result of left-sided heart valve disease and has historically
been managed conservatively despite an association with
significant morbidity and mortality.12,13 In general, the
tricuspid leaflets in patients with functional TR are morphologically normal. Thus, significant annular dilatation (with
or without leaflet tethering) is the usual mechanism that
leads to inadequate leaflet coaptation resulting in varying
degrees of central regurgitation. Like the MV, the TV has
a complex anatomic structure that includes a saddleshaped annulus that changes shape in systole and diastole,
3 leaflets, chordae and papillary muscles, and underlying

crescent-shaped right ventricular myocardium. Competency of the TV depends on interplay of all components
as well as loading conditions (preload/afterload). Thus,
functional TR is highly dynamic and may vary widely in
the same patient depending on volume loading and hemodynamic conditions. As such, TR may distinctively be
downgraded in fasting patients under general anesthesia.
Assessment of the degree of annular dilatation as an indicator for TVr was initially described by Carpentier and
colleagues14 who compared valve leaflet surface area with
the annular diameter. Dreyfus and colleagues15 later popularized an intraoperative measurement of 70 mm as a
threshold for valve repair. The current consensus echocardiographic guideline threshold defines significant tricuspid
annular dilatation as an end-diastolic diameter of 40 mm (or
>21 mm/m2), and this has been supported by several
retrospective series.7
Growing evidence suggests that functional TR does not
completely resolve after successful mitral repair.13 In fact,
mild or moderate degrees of functional TR have been shown

left-sided valve surgery have been identified. These include

the time of MVr for degenerative disease. In that study, patients who underwent combined TVr/MVr enjoyed
improved short-term survival (2-year) and a lower incidence of TR progression or tricuspid reoperation compared
with those who had only TVr alone.22 Nevertheless, despite
these findings, as well as standard guideline recommendations, the frequency of combined TVr at the time of MVr
is ambiguous and may range from 5% to 75%.1,23
In many ways, TVr can be as challenging as MVr with
more fragile leaflet/annular tissue and the severity of right
heart dilatation can make successful leaflet coaptation and
competency more difficult. Additionally, TV surgery
carries intrinsic risk with coronary and/or conduction tissue
injury. In general, banded annuloplasty, as opposed to suture annuloplasty, is the preferred approach for annular
reduction in patients with functional TR given improved
durability.24 We have previously reported our technique
for open TVr.25 Exact replications of each step of conventional “open” TVr with the robotic platform was followed
in the present study to replicate traditional repair techniques

and outcomes. In open cases, we typically use a 26-mm partial ring routinely and a 28-mm partial ring in larger patients
(>100 kg). However, in the present series, we primarily
used a 63-mm band (89% of cases) given ease of band flexibility and implantation. The annuloplasty technique we
follow begins at the anteroseptal junction and moves clockwise along the anterior and inferior annulus. The last stitch
is pledgeted and anchored into the ostium of the coronary
sinus thus avoiding extending the band across the septal
annulus thereby minimizing potential injury to the conduction tissue. Additionally, we believe anchoring the band in
the coronary sinus ensures that the band stays intact,
whereas more superficial bites at or near the inferoseptal
junction are susceptible to dehiscence.
Since the introduction of robotic techniques for MVr in
the late 1990s by Carpentier, Nifong, and Chitwood,
robotic-assisted MVr has been established as safe, effective,
comparable, and durable approach for degenerative disease.26-28 Unfortunately, safety, efficacy, and overall
results of combined robotic-assisted MVr and TVr have
not been rigorously evaluated and widespread adoption of
such techniques are still forthcoming. Among the earliest
reports of robotic MV and TV work was by Jones and colleagues.29 In that study of 32 patients with roboticassisted MVr, only 4 had concomitant TVr and 1 of those
patients experienced early hospital mortality. Mori and colleagues30 found a 5.8% rate of concomitant TV repair in
degenerative MR using the robotic platform, compared
with a 16% rate during a sternotomy approach in a matched
cohort. Critics of the robotic approach suggest that surgeons
may not as frequently perform concomitant TVr due to the
added complexity of the procedure. In our study, there was a
significant increase in cardiopulmonary bypass and crossclamp time in the concomitant group. However, nearly all

patients (17 out of 18) with preoperative AF in the combined MVr/TVr group underwent concomitant Cox-maze

Additionally, more complex mitral pathologies, including

In 2014, Lewis and colleagues31 reported 1 of the largest series of 50 patients with combined robotic-assisted MV and
TV interventions. These were carefully selected patients
with relatively straightforward valve pathology (functional
MR and TR) amenable to simple repairs. They showed
improvement in crossclamp and bypass times over a
5-year period with acceptable surgical results and low mortality (2 early reoperations and 2 deaths in the cohort).
In the present study, we show that concomitant roboticassisted TVr can be applied in settings of more complex
MV disease: >30% of MVrs in the cohort were composed
of either anterior leaflet or bileaflet (Barlow’s type) pathologies. We also add to the repertoire of additional concomitant procedures such as closure of patent foramen ovale, left
atrial appendage closure, and maze procedures that were
performed in up to one-third of patients with no added
risk of morbidity. In comparison to isolated roboticassisted MVr, our findings affirm that robotic MVr and
TVr do not adversely influence quality outcomes and excellent short-term survival and freedom from reoperation can
be obtained and expected.
There are several potential advantages of adopting
robotic-assisted MVr and TVr versus sternotomy or even
thoracoscopic approaches. First, surgical dexterity with
the robot is significantly enhanced as compared with the
fulcrum effect that limits more precise movements when
utilizing endoscopic instruments with a long shaft. Highdefinition and 3-dimensional visualization allows robotic
surgeons unparalleled visibility and maneuverability within
the atrial chambers. Superior cosmesis and improved postsurgical outcomes have been reported, including shorter
length of hospitalization/intensive care unit time, and fewer
wound infections.32 Reduced surgical trauma from avoidance of sternotomy has translated into reduced requirements of heterologous blood products, fewer incidences
of postoperative AGF, and improvement in postoperative
pain.33 Experienced robotic MVr groups have reported
collectively on several thousands of patients with hospital
mortality rates <0.9%, stroke rate of 0.6% to 1.7%, and
re-exploration for bleeding of 2.2% to 4.7%. Further, total
hospital cost associated with robotic-assisted valve repair
has been reported to be similar to that of conventional sternotomy ($30 606 vs $31 310), in high-volume centers.8,34
The limitations of the present study include a retrospective study design within a single center with inherent selection bias of patients and procedures. Nearly all patients (17

CONCLUSIONS
Our study demonstrates that concomitant roboticassisted TVr at the time of MVr in patients with moderate
TR and/or significant annular dilatation can be performed
safely and effectively with acceptable low morbidity and
mortality. Longitudinal follow-up is warranted to validate
these findings over an extended period of time.
