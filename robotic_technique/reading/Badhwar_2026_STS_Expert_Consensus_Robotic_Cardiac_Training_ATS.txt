outlines principles for the safe initial introduction and subsequent expansion of robotic cardiac programs. The 25-year history of robotic cardiac surgery has established safety and efficacy while

and evidence to inform a recent global surge in interest in incorporating robotic techniques into standard cardiac surgical practice. This consensus is a collaborative effort between the STS Workforce on
Evidence Based Surgery, the STS Robotic Cardiac Surgery Taskforce, and multinational leaders in robotic cardiac surgery that aims to standardize initial core principles of preparatory elements, followed
by 4 phases of robotic cardiac training to proceed from program commencement to mastery.

BUILDING A FOUNDATION

Although surgeon skill and team building are
essential to any successfully sustainable robotic
cardiac program, so too is access to a robot for clinical
use. As is true for any other tool or technique, if robotic cardiac surgery is underused or under
accessed, the team’s skill and proficiency will
decline. At a foundational level, any program
wishing to embark on robotic cardiac surgery should
have secured institutional administrative support
for access to operative robot time. An established
and well-functioning heart team is suggested to
ensure multidisciplinary support. It is recommended for developing programs to ideally have
robotic access 1 to 2 days per week during standard
hours, but at a minimum of twice per month. Team
building to include scrub and circulators, table-side
associates, cardiac anesthesia, and perfusion, are
all essential. Every effort to ensure team consistency
in the initial phases of progress is critical to secure
cumulative learning and development.25

PHASES OF PREPARATION AND ADVANCEMENT

The following aims to outline phases of
advancement in robotic cardiac training (Figure).

These phases are recommendations for team
elevation, sustainable quality, and to facilitate
an evolution of procedural complexity. Volume
recommendations to follow serve as minimums,
with recognition that specific countries with
different structures of centralization may wish
to increase these thresholds. At each step, the
most important goal is securing progress while
maintaining optimal outcomes of mortality and
major morbidity, including need for reoperation
based on institutional and national comparative
metrics to open procedures. Observed-toexpected (O/E) outcomes may be derived from
the STS isolated coronary artery bypass grafting
(CABG) or relevant STS valve risk calculator or
international equivalent.

PHASE I
PHASE I—BASIC PREPARATION. Successful initiation of a

robotic cardiac surgery program requires deliberate
team building focused on multidisciplinary coordination: a lead cardiac robotic surgeon with prior minimally
invasive
experience,
dedicated
anesthesiologists familiar with single-lung ventilation
and TEE, specially trained bedside cosurgeon,
associate surgeons or assistants, scrub nurses,
perfusionists, and institutional administrative
support for scheduling and equipment maintenance.
A structured team-training pathway—combining
simulation, dry laboratory practice, and stepwise
supervised clinical cases—reduces the learning curve
and helps maintain patient safety during the
program’s ramp-up phase.24,25
The primary goal of phase I is the fundamental
surgeon and surgical team training on use of the
robot. This includes table-side thoracic preparation and port placement, console surgeon robotic
manufacturer-specific telemanipulation, including
clutching and conflict management, and team
docking, troubleshooting, and maintenance.
Rehearsal and team preparation for safe conversion to minithoracotomy or sternotomy should
also be covered. Phase I is often best facilitated by
simulation or thoracic models, and this may be
enhanced by artificial tissue or cadaveric

simulation. Phase I may be completed with the
assistance of a facilitator experienced in robotic
team training or training coordinators often associated with the robotic manufacturer.

PHASE II

After successful completion of phase I and necessary stakeholder communication and workflow
preparation, phase II includes peer-to-peer clinical
training and initial proctored cases. After phase I
team training at a program’s institution, phase II
peer-to-peer training should be coordinated to
include site visit(s) to an established robotic
institution that has achieved mastery (eg, phase
III-IV) with the capability of training not only the
console surgeon but also additional training of the
table-side surgeon associate as well as all other
members of the team as optional additions. The
decision to have another attending-level surgeon
or physician assistant perform table-side assistance should be up to each institution and team
such that the table-side experience is sufficient to
achieve optimal efficiency and patient safety.
Phase II training should include case observation specific to the track desired for program
commencement, which may include both tracks
should the initiating program have the necessary
access and case volumes to support both. Should
the peer-to-peer host institutions have the capability, a separate and structured training experience dedicated to team members would be of
benefit (eg, table side, perfusion, cardiac anesthesia, scrub, circulator). These may include
specific sharing of equipment and pick lists,
setup, patient positioning, instrumentation, and
perfusion, as applicable.

PHASE III

Phase III is defined as achieving safe completion of
robotic cardiac surgery with completion of the first
50 cases in either track with O/E mortality and
major morbidity ≤1, and with improving efficiency
of operative and intraprocedural times. Evaluating
team and case efficiency using metrics such as
total operative time or technical or anatomic outcomes is currently insufficient to enable effective
learning and team progression. Because each
member of the team can develop unique skills and
training to improve the efficient and safe performance of a robotic operation, a gradation in team
proficiency can be measured more effectively by
compartmentalizing operative steps and tracking
each over time. Common steps that can be timed,
tracked, and optimized for either robotic cardiac
pathway may include (1) operative table setup, (2)
patient operating room arrival, anesthetic induction including catheters (wheels in to anesthesia
release), (3) robot sterile draping, (4) patient positioning and topographic marking, (5) skin incision
to port placement completion, (6) robotic docking
from resting room position to table side lock, and
(7) robotic port locking and initial instrument
positioning until console control has been
established.
All programs are recommended to use this
compartmentalized evaluation, or similar components tailored to the individual institution, to
track times and share at the conclusion of each
case or at an appropriate interval for shared
learning. It is further recommended that robotic
operating room teams prebrief and debrief with
regularity to track shared learning and optimize
workflow.

PHASE IV

Phase IV is the final phase of robotic cardiac
training, once programmatic safety, quality, and
procedural proficiency have been achieved after
phase III. The case minimum to enter phase IV
should be at least 50 cases; however, this may
require more cases if case frequency is challenging based on hospital or referral practices.
One important objective signal of proficiency is
achievement of the steps outlined in phase III
that beats the target times by a significant margin
and other team times plateau with consistency.
If a mutually supportive relationship can be
nurtured, along with the skill of the robotic team,
programmatic advancement and introduction of
new techniques can be readily achieved.3,5,6,33-35
Overall, the objectives of phase IV are to advance
to the desires of the program by adopting additional techniques of each track, such as TECAB in
the revascularization track and multivalve (mitral
and tricuspid), multivalve Cox maze, and right
lateral transaxillary robotic aortic valve replacement for the intracardiac track. Additionally, for

isolated or nonaortic procedures, should the
programs believe that percutaneous cannulation
with endoballoon technology provides positional
advantages, phase IV would the appropriate time
to explore its use for programs that have not had
experience with it previously. Similarly, this
would also be the time should programs wish to
proceed with near totally endoscopic techniques
for isolated valve or intracardiac operations that
incorporate endoballoon technology and antegrade cardioplegia.
Additionally, phase IV would be the appropriate time to address using the program’s robotic
experience to approach higher comorbid risk or
frail patients who may not tolerate a sternotomy.
This is a particularly valuable opportunity once
teams have reached phase IV proficiency because
this may also be an accelerant for safe programmatic expansion. Redo cardiac operations may
also be an option during phase IV, provided the
operative principles remain the same. Although
redo procedures can be performed with transthoracic clamping in certain circumstances, these
may also be achieved with endoballoon technology or by ventricular fibrillatory arrest in patients
with a competent aortic valve, and all with
acceptable levels of safety.
Finally, approaching advanced pathoanatomy
is recommended for phase IV. This may include
bileaflet or Barlow’s mitral pathology, endocarditis repair with autologous patches, concomitant septal myectomy, or approaching patients
with both complex anatomy and impaired ejection fraction. All in all, the objective in phase IV is
that programs achieve a level of mastery while
continuing to track metrics of outcomes and
