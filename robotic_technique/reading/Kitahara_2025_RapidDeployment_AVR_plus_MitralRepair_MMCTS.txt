## Abstract

Robotic cardiac surgery continues to evolve, offering patients a less invasive alternative to traditional median sternotomy, even for complex procedures. This report presents a case of robotic totally endoscopic aortic valve replacement using a rapid deployment valve combined with mitral valve repair, demonstrating the feasibility and safety of this approach.

## Introduction

Minimally invasive cardiac surgery for double valve disease, aortic valve and mitral valve remains technically demanding and is performed by only a limited number of surgeons. Robotic technology provides enhanced visualization, allowing for a totally endoscopic approach in such complex surgery. We demonstrate the safety and feasibility of robotic totally endoscopic aortic valve replacement using a rapid deployment valve and mitral valve repair.

## Patient presentation

The patient is a 65-year-old woman with known aortic valve insufficiency who came to our institution seeking a minimally invasive surgical option. Trans-oesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction and severe aortic insufficiency. There was severe mitral regurgitation due to annular dilatation as well. CT showed no atherosclerosis in the aorta. Robotic totally endoscopic double valve surgery was performed.

## Surgical technique

### 1. 1 - Case presentation  [17]

The patient is a 65-year-old woman with known aortic valve insufficiency who came to our institution seeking a minimally invasive surgical option. Trans-oesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed preserved LV ejection fraction and severe aortic insufficiency. There was severe mitral regurgitation due to annular dilatation as well. CT showed no atherosclerosis in the aorta. Robotic totally endoscopic double valve surgery was performed.

### 2. 2 - Port placement  [61]

The patient is placed supine with a roll under the right chest. General anaesthesia is induced with a single-lumen endotracheal tube. With the lungs deflated, a Verres needle is inserted in the third intercostal space to insufflate CO2 in the right pleural space. An 8-mm camera port is placed in the third intercostal space. An 8-mm robotic port is placed in the anterior fifth intercostal space and a 14-gauge angiocath needle is inserted next to it under direct camera vision. The camera is moved to the fifth intercostal space port. A right arm port is placed in the fifth intercostal space in the mid-axillary line. Two percutaneous suction tubes are inserted in the fourth and fifth intercostal space. A left arm port is placed in the second intercostal space in the anterior-axillary line. The original third intercostal camera port is removed and a soft tissue retractor is placed in the same space and used as an 8-mm working port. The working port is closed off with wet gauze to maintain CO2 pressure inside. A new camera port is placed anterior to the working port in the same intercostal space. Two needles are placed in the second and fourth intercostal space for pericardial retraction. A transthoracic aortic clamp is inserted in the second intercostal space in the mid-axillary line. A 14-gauge antegrade cardioplegia needle is inserted in the second intercostal space in the mid-clavicular line.

### 3. 3 - Set-up for cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp  [149]

Cardiopulmonary bypass is established with femoral arterial and venous cannulation. The pericardium is opened 2 cm anterior to the phrenic nerve and extended toward the end of the pericardial reflection. Two pericardial retraction sutures are placed and brought out to the skin by the needles. The aorta is retracted by the robotic dynamic retractor and dissected from the right pulmonary artery. The camera is switched to 30° down, and the aorta is dissected from the main pulmonary artery as well. The trans-thoracic clamp is inserted through the dissected space and a cross-clamp is applied. The antegrade cardioplegia needle is inserted in the ascending aorta and del-Nido cardioplegia is delivered.

### 4. 4 - Mitral valve repair  [194]

The left atrium is opened and a suction tube is placed into the left upper pulmonary vein. The mitral valve is tested with water injection, and the annulus size is measured. A 36-mm semi-rigid annuloplasty band is chosen. The band is brought in through the 8-mm working port. The band is implanted with a 3-0 Prolene continuous mattress running suture technique. The first mattress suture starts from the left commissure and ends at the left one-third of the posterior annulus. The second suture starts from the right commissure and ends where the first suture stopped. Both sutures are tied with a Cor-Knot device. The mitral valve is tested and residual leakage is found at the P2 leaflet. The cleft in the P2 leaflet is closed using 5-0 single interrupted suture. There is no significant residual mitral regurgitation. The sump suction tube is placed deep in the left ventricle for future venting.

### 5. 5 - Aortic valve replacement using rapid-deployment valve  [271]

The antegrade cardioplegia catheter is removed and transverse aortotomy is made where it was inserted. The aortic valve leaflets and annular calcifications are sharply resected using robotic scissors. The valve is irrigated with saline injection to clear debris. The left arm port is temporally removed and the port incision is extended using Hegar dilators (12–19 mm) to accommodate the size of the rapid-deployment valve sizer. A soft tissue retractor is placed in the left arm port incision. The sizer is inserted through the incision and the left arm port is re-inserted. The annulus size is measured with several sizers, and a medium size rapid-deployment valve is chosen. Stay sutures are placed in the nadir of each cusp. The sutures are brought out through the left arm port incision.

While additional cardioplegia is selectively delivered to the left and right coronary artery, the sutures are placed in the rapid-deployment valve. The left arm port is removed again. The valve is introduced through the left arm working port and deployed under vision using the robotic camera. After its correct position is confirmed by direct inspection, the valve is balloon-expanded for 30 seconds at 4 psi. The aortotomy is closed with a 4-0 double-layer running suture technique. Before closing the aortotomy completely, the left atriotomy is closed with 4-0 running suture leaving the suction tube inside the left ventricle. Extensive de-airing is performed with a gentle Valsalva manoeuvre. The aortotomy is closed completely, the aortic cross-clamp is released and haemostasis is confirmed in the aortotomy closure. After extensive de-airing, the LV suction tube is removed and the left atriotomy is closed. Postoperative TEE shows a well-seated rapid-deployment aortic valve and no paravalvular leakage. The mean pressure gradient of the aortic valve is 5 mmHg. Mitral valve leaflets move well with no significant residual mitral regurgitation.

## Outcome and discussion

Minimally invasive surgery for combined aortic and mitral valve disease has been performed only in selected high-volume centres [1]. Outcomes of the mini-thoracotomy approach for double valve surgery have been shown to be comparable to those achieved via median sternotomy [2]. To further reduce surgical trauma and improve visualization, totally endoscopic double valve surgery has been successfully performed with the aid of three-dimensional endoscopic systems [3]. More recently, Chen et al. conducted a comparative study evaluating totally endoscopic versus sternotomy approaches for double valve surgery, involving 76 totally endoscopic and 77 sternotomy cases [4]. Despite a longer cardiopulmonary bypass time in the endoscopic group, postoperative outcomes including shorter ventilation time, shorter length of hospital stay, and lower blood transfusion requirements favoured the endoscopic approach.

To further enhance minimally invasive capabilities, robotic technology has been increasingly utilized, particularly in mitral valve surgery, where it is now widely standardized [5]. However, robotic approaches for combined double valve procedures remain uncommon [6]. In this report, we applied our standard robotic mitral valve repair approach to a combined robotic totally endoscopic double valve procedure. A key distinction in our technique was the use of a rapid deployment valve, which allowed for a smaller incision and reduced operative time. Previous studies have demonstrated that rapid deployment valves offer equivalent outcomes compared to conventional sutured valves [7,8]. In our case, the largest incision was limited to 20 mm, required for the introduction of the valve sizer. With further improvements in device design, particularly in valve sizer, this incision may be further minimized.

In conclusion, robotic totally endoscopic rapid deployment aortic valve replacement combined with mitral valve repair can be safely and effectively performed at our centre. The robotic technology could enhance the applicability of minimally invasive treatment for complex double valve disease.

## Summary

Robotic totally endoscopic aortic valve replacement using a rapid deployment valve combined with mitral valve repair is feasible and safe, offering the least invasive approach for double valve disease.
