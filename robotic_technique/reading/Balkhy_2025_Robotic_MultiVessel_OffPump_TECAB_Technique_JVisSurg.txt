Abstract: Robotic totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass (TECAB) is a highly advanced yet
evolving surgical technique for coronary artery disease. While it is not yet a standard procedure, ongoing
developments in robotic technology have positioned it as a promising option for the future. TECAB allows
for multi-vessel coronary artery bypass using bilateral internal mammary artery (BIMA) grafts in a completely
minimally invasive, off-pump approach. With over 15 years of experience performing TECAB, we have
refined patient selection criteria and surgical techniques. Initially, pleural scarring or diffuse coronary artery
disease were considered contraindications. However, the precision and magnification provided by robotic
platforms have enabled us to expand indications, including for redo surgeries where internal mammary artery
grafts are still viable. While the operation is typically limited to left coronary artery bypass, recent cases
have demonstrated its feasibility for right coronary artery branches under specific conditions. We believe
that the off-pump approach in this setting has significant advantages for early patient recovery and return
to full function. While technically demanding we have demonstrated excellent graft patency and long-term
outcomes with this procedure. In this video, we present our current technique for robotic multivessel offpump TECAB using the da Vinci Si surgical system. We will outline patient presentation, surgical steps, and
key technical insights to demonstrate how this minimally invasive approach continues to evolve and expand
its clinical utility.

Introduction
Coronary artery disease remains a leading cause of
mortality worldwide. Standard surgical approaches, such
as open coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), involve
sternotomy, which leads to prolonged recovery times and

higher morbidity. Totally endoscopic coronary artery bypass
(TECAB) addresses these concerns by enabling off-pump,
robotic grafting using bilateral internal mammary artery
(BIMA) conduits in a totally endoscopic approach. This
approach improves patient recovery and reduces hospital

What is conventional and what is novel/modified?
• BIMA grafting is a well-established technique in coronary artery
bypass grafting, typically performed via median sternotomy and
often with cardiopulmonary bypass.
• This procedure represents a novel application of conventional
principles by achieving complete arterial revascularization through
five small incisions in a totally endoscopic, sternal-sparing, offpump approach. The use of robotic assistance to perform handsewn anastomoses entirely endoscopically—without sternotomy or
cardiopulmonary bypass—is a major technical innovation.
What is the implication, and what should change now?
• This technique demonstrates that complete multivessel arterial
revascularization can be accomplished using a minimally invasive,
robotic, off-pump strategy—avoiding the morbidity of sternotomy
while maintaining surgical quality. As robotic platforms evolve and
new stabilizers become available, this approach may become more
widely adopted in select centers committed to advanced minimally
invasive cardiac surgery.

Patient presentation
A 64-year-old male with hypertension, non-insulin
dependent diabetes, chronic kidney disease, stage 3,
and gout who presented to our clinic with fatigue and
dyspnea on exertion on moderate activities. His left heart
catheterization shows significant stenosis on his proximal
(90%) LAD and mid (80%) left circumflex arteries. Given
his comorbidities and anatomy, robotic multivessel TECAB
was selected.
Anesthesia, patient position, and port placement
The patient was positioned supine with a right radial arterial
line for monitoring, transesophageal echocardiography
(TEE), and external defibrillator pads placed on the left
scapular region and the right lateral chest. A single-lumen
endotracheal tube with an endobronchial blocker was
utilized to allow split-lung ventilation. CO 2 insufflation
was employed to maintain space within the thoracic cavity
during the procedure. If the patient could not tolerate
right lung-only ventilation, partial left lung ventilation
was achieved by releasing the blocker, with CO2 pressure
maintaining thoracic working space.

The patient was positioned with a small roll under the
left shoulder, and port placements were made in the 2nd
(right arm), 4th (camera), and 6th (left arm) intercostal spaces,
medial to the anterior axillary line. CO2 insufflation at
8–12 mmHg created working space within the thoracic
cavity after docking the robotic patient cart on the patient’s
right side.

 A 12-mm service port in the 2 intercostal space at

Pericardiotomy and mediastinum fat dissection
Using a Maryland bipolar forceps in the left arm and
monopolar curved scissors in the right arm with a 30° upscope, the posterior pericardiotomy was made posterior to
the phrenic nerve to access the obtuse marginal artery. The
posterior pericardiotomy also aids postoperative drainage
and is performed routinely in TECAB procedures. Then,
the anterior mediastinal fat was dissected and reflected
laterally for adequate exposure. An anterior pericardiotomy
was performed to visualize the LAD artery.
BIMA takedown
Fat dissection extended under the sternum to the right
pleural space, exposing the right internal mammary artery
(RIMA). Using a permanent cautery spatula (right arm) and
micro bipolar forceps (left arm), the RIMA was harvested
first with a skeletonized approach. Small branches were
divided with bipolar cautery, while larger branches were
clipped. The EndoWrist stabilizer was introduced to
enhance exposure during RIMA takedown.
The LIMA was harvested similarly, from above the
first intercostal artery to its distal bifurcation. Post-LIMA
takedown, the surgeon placed the subcostal and service
ports and used the EndoWrist stabilizer to facilitate
proximal and distal RIMA dissection. Meticulous hemostasis
of the IMA bed was ensured throughout.
Preparation of target vessel
Target vessel preparation was performed with a 30° downscope, a permanent cautery spatula (right arm), and

micro bipolar forceps (left arm). Epicardial dissection was
conducted with low-intensity cautery to minimize bleeding.
The right arm was equipped with a large needle driver to
place a SaddleLoop snare. The target vessel was inspected
to ensure it was free of significant atherosclerosis or
calcification at the anastomotic site.
Preparation of internal mammary artery
Intravenous heparin was administered to achieve an
activated clotting time of 300–350 seconds. A bulldog
clamp attached to a suture was used to occlude the LIMA,
which was bisected distally with Potts scissors. Papaverine
(60 mg in 20 mL saline) was injected into the LIMA using
a 20-Ga Perifix epidural catheter to ensure vasodilation and
prevent spasms. The catheter was introduced through the
service port and advanced 15–20 cm into the LIMA before
injection. The procedure was repeated for the RIMA,
ensuring adequate preparation for anastomosis.
Hand-sewn anastomosis
The in-situ RIMA was directed to the LAD coronary
artery. The LIMA was grafted to the first obtuse marginal
(OM1).
Anastomosis sites were stabilized with the EndoWrist
stabilizer. Ischemic preconditioning was achieved by
snaring the proximal coronary artery for 5–8 minutes while
instruments were prepared. A coronary arteriotomy was
created with a Snap-Fit endo-knife, extended with Potts
scissors, and stabilized with a shunt. A running 7-0 Pronova
suture was used for the anastomosis, and the shunt was
removed just before tying.
Transit time flowmetry (TTFM) was performed for each
graft using a MediStim flow meter to confirm satisfactory
flow and a low pulsatility index. Graft revision was
considered for flow rates below 15 mL/min or pulsatility
indices above 5, with careful evaluation of target quality.
Covering IMA grafts and drain placement
Protamine was administered after graft measurements.
Reflected pericardial fat was sutured to the medial
pericardium using 4-0 V-Loc suture to cover and protect
the grafts. A 24-Fr Blake drain was placed in the right
pleural cavity via the subcostal port, and another drain was
positioned in the left pleural cavity through the left arm
port. Suturing only the medial edge of the pericardium

Postoperative considerations and tasks
Postoperative course
The patient’s recovery was uneventful, highlighting the
benefits of minimally invasive robotic surgery. He was
extubated on the day of surgery (postoperative day 0),
had chest tubes removed on postoperative day 1, and was
discharged home comfortably on postoperative day 2. He
was started on aspirin and Plavix therapy for 2 months. The
patient underwent follow-up at 1 month postoperatively
with repeat chest X-ray and ECG. He was symptoms free
and back to his normal activity.
Tips and pearls
Successful execution of robotic multivessel TECAB
hinges on meticulous planning, team coordination, and
familiarity with robotic instrumentation. Key pearls include
harvesting both IMAs with a skeletonized technique to
maximize conduit length, ensuring optimal port placement
to avoid arm collision and facilitate exposure, and
performing pericardiotomy early to improve visualization
and drainage. Maintaining low CO2 insufflation pressures
minimizes hemodynamic compromise. To support
hemodynamics during cardiac manipulation, favor the use
of vasoconstrictors such as phenylephrine and adequate
volume resuscitation, while avoiding inotropes when
possible. Finally, routinely using transit-time flowmetry
helps confirm graft patency intraoperatively and provides
immediate quality assurance.
Discussion
Robotic TECAB has emerged as a highly specialized
yet promising approach for multivessel coronary
revascularization. Compared to traditional sternotomybased CABG, TECAB offers distinct advantages, including
avoidance of sternal complications, reduced postoperative
pain, quicker recovery, and improved cosmetic outcomes—
all without compromising graft patency or clinical
effectiveness (2,3).
In this case, robotic multivessel TECAB was selected
for a patient with significant LAD and circumflex disease
and multiple comorbidities, including diabetes and chronic
kidney disease—factors often associated with elevated risk

for sternal wound complications in open surgery. The use
of BITAs in such patients would be traditionally limited
due to the increased risk of deep sternal wound infection.
However, by avoiding sternotomy, robotic TECAB allows
for liberal use of BITA even in high-risk patients, a strategy
shown to confer improved long-term survival and lower
rates of MACCE and repeat revascularization (4,5).
Our institutional experience of nearly 1,000 robotic
TECAB cases, including more than 450 multivessel
procedures with BITA, has demonstrated that with a
dedicated team and standardized technique, outcomes can
be excellent—even in complex patients. In published series,
perioperative mortality in robotic CABG has been reported
as low as 0.8%, with LITA-LAD patency exceeding 95%
at 1 year (6,7). Similarly, multivessel TECAB using BITA
has been shown to achieve patency rates and completeness
of revascularization comparable to conventional CABG,
especially when hybrid strategies [with adjunct percutaneous
coronary intervention (PCI)] are employed where
appropriate (8).
Nevertheless, the procedure remains technically
demanding and requires significant institutional experience
and team consistency to achieve optimal results. Operative
time is often longer than conventional CABG, particularly
early in the learning curve, but tends to decrease with
growing familiarity. Importantly, the robotic approach
enables precise dissection and anastomosis under highdefinition three-dimensional (3D) visualization, which is
especially advantageous in patients with difficult anatomy or
heavy calcification (9).
This case reflects not only the feasibility but also the
expanding scope of robotic TECAB in coronary surgery.
As experience accumulates, this approach is becoming a
valuable alternative to conventional CABG, particularly in
selected patients where minimally invasive, sternal-sparing
surgery offers clinical benefit without compromising graft
durability or completeness of revascularization.

Quality control measures:
 Intraoperative verification of graft patency using
transit-time flowmetry;
 Minimization of electrocautery near coronary
targets to avoid thermal injury.
Safety considerations: the primary intraoperative risks
included hemodynamic instability and graft injury. These
were mitigated by:
 Maintaining normotensive and euvolemia during
graft anastomosis;
 Avoiding excessive CO 2 insufflation pressures
(>12 mmHg);
 Having a perfusion team on standby for emergency
conversion to on-pump CABG.
Impact and cost:
 While robotic systems have high initial costs, the
reduced hospital stay and faster recovery offset
expenses over time. Future advancements in
robotic platforms will likely improve accessibility
and further refine the technique.
Conclusions
For surgical teams adopting robotic TECAB, it is essential
to understand that outcomes improve progressively with
experience. The most critical factors for successfully
performing off-pump TECAB are meticulous
intraoperative management—particularly maintaining
patient hemodynamics—effective hemostasis, and strong
communication between the surgeon and the operative
team. As robotic technology and techniques continue to
advance, this procedure will likely become more widely
adopted as a cornerstone of minimally invasive coronary
artery surgery.
