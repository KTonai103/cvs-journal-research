Optimal exposure in mitral valve surgery is crucial for procedural success. Robotic techniques provide unparalleled visualization when ports and retraction sutures are positioned precisely. Here, we outline our standardized approach for patient positioning, marking and port placement, derived from extensive institutional experience. Adherence to this technique reliably ensures excellent valve exposure and facilitates reproducible surgical outcomes.

In robotic mitral valve repair, exposure is crucial—often just a few centimetres of variation can be the difference between a straightforward procedure and a challenging one. The converse is also true: well-positioned ports and retraction sutures can transform a complex procedure into a manageable one, whether the difficulty arises from the patient’s body habitus or the complexity of the valve repair itself. Correct port placement begins with careful patient positioning and accurate marking, particularly of the anterior axillary line, upon which the majority of ports are based. While there are guiding principles, some aspects of positioning rely on a sense of what feels right, rather than strict science. Though the intuitive approach is valuable, it can also lead to overthinking—especially in lower-volume centres or when first adopting robotic techniques. Standardizing the approach and minimizing variation from case to case is essential for building consistency and achieving reliable outcomes. The method outlined below will result in optimal exposure for most male patients.

- 1 - Marking and positioning (0:19)
	Optimal exposure begins with precise patient marking. It is particularly important to be able to rely on the accuracy of the sternotomy mark in case of the occasional need for conversion to sternotomy, as the correct position for robotic approach will result in an off-midline incision if trying to ascertain this only at the time. While being midline is always important, it is even more so in patients who are often expecting the cosmetic result associated with minimally invasive surgery, in addition to its other benefits.
	We mark the sternotomy with the patient still in the supine position and with the arms by their side, followed by the fourth intercostal space (ICS) counting down from the manubriosternal junction at the second ICS. We also mark the nipple to prevent inadvertent injury later once the drapes are on and its visualization becomes more difficult. Finally, we mark the femoral incision point, midway between the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS)–pubic tubercle line and the groin crease, crossing the femoral pulse. If a high femoral bifurcation is noted on preoperative computed tomography (CT), ultrasound confirmation is performed to ensure that we are at the level of the common femoral artery and not more distal. Our choice of side is influenced if one side is higher than the other, or if one side has some atherosclerosis while the other does not. All things being equal we prefer to use the right side as it is easier to insert the venous cannula from here.
	The patient is then moved over towards the right side of the bed, and a rolled towel is placed below the scapula to allow the right arm to drop backwards, enhancing exposure of the axilla. The arm is secured using a custom sled, a sheet and a foam sling, with extra foam pieces under the shoulder for support.
	The deltopectoral groove is marked followed by the anterior axillary line. This line is crucial as all incisions are based on it. A 4 cm working port is marked in the fourth ICS crossing the anterior axillary line, with 1/3 in front and 2/3 behind it. Diaphragmatic retraction is planned in the sixth ICS anterior to the anterior axillary line, the right arm in the sixth ICS posterior to the anterior axillary line, and the left arm anterior to the anterior axillary line in the third ICS (we find it unnecessary to use the second in the vast majority of male patients). The basket vent entry point is marked posterior to the main incision.
- 2 - Femoral exposure (3:08)
	Next, a 3 cm incision is made to access the common femoral artery and vein. Electrocautery is used for dissection to ensure a bloodless field. We dissect minimally to expose just the anterior surface of the vessels, and will later cannulate within a purse-string using the Seldinger technique under transoesophageal echocardiogram (TOE) guidance.
	The purse-string suture is tied at the end of the case, and the vessels oversewn in case of any bleeding. We also aim to tie off any lymph nodes to minimize the risk of seroma formation, though this is rare due to limited dissection.
- 3 - Working port (3:43)
	Next, we make a 4 cm incision crossing the anterior axillary line to create the working port. Meticulous haemostasis is ensured once again. The lateral border of the pectoralis major is identified and swept medially, with the retractor then moved deeper.
	We then identify the serratus anterior muscle and carefully divide its fibres, preserving the muscle. As the rib begins to be exposed, we confirm that we are in the correct ICS, using the mark medial to the nipple as the most reliable surface guide.
	Once confirmed, we enter the pleural space bluntly with the lungs fully disconnected. We allow a short time for the right lung to deflate before recommencing left lung ventilation and completing the incision with electrocautery for haemostasis.
- 4 - Angiocath placement and diaphragm retraction (5:23)
	Three tiny stab incisions are made for the placement of angiocaths for retraction sutures: one anterior to the anterior axillary line over the seventh rib, and two posteriorly, usually over the eighth and fifth ribs. These positions vary slightly depending on the patient and the surgeon, with the aim being simply to place them posteriorly with one as far to the head and the other as far to the feet as possible within the safe limits of the length of the surgeon’s finger.
	We use limited Bovie cautery for coagulation and proceed to insert the angiocaths blindly. To safely enter the chest, we push the diaphragm away with the left hand while advancing the needle, walking up the rib and then slowly advancing into the pleural space. For the posterior angiocaths, we can feel the entry point more clearly; we carefully advance the needle through the intercostal muscle on top of the rib, then push the lung away to allow the needle to be advanced sufficiently far to prevent inadvertent placement of the soft catheter portion, which is shorter than the needle, in the extrapleural space. These posterior angiocaths are not required until after we open the pericardium later in the case, but it is much easier to put them in at this stage before the soft tissue retractor is in position.
	A diaphragmatic retraction stitch is placed next, carefully identifying the central tendon of the diaphragm. A shallow initial bite is taken first to avoid the liver, which is then tented up to allow a more generous second bite. This is tied with a knot pusher and the ends of the stitch retrieved through the angiocath and secured within it with artery forceps. The stitch should be pulled in a slow gentle fashion to minimize the risk of liver bleeding.
- 5 - Port placement and completed setup (7:39)
	We position the right and left arm ports based loosely on the previously marked locations, making adjustments to ensure the angle of entry is correct. The goal is to create sufficient distance between the robotic arms, as well as ensuring the correct angle of each arm directed toward the working area inside the heart, thus avoiding conflict between the arms during the procedure. Additionally, a fourth small incision is made for the basket vent close to the main incision.
	We almost always use a long port for the right arm to avoid inadvertent injury to the diaphragm or liver during instrument exchanges. For the left atrial (LA) retractor and the left arm, short ports are typically sufficient. It is important to pay attention to the angle of insertion of the right and left arms to ensure they are pointing toward the mitral valve area. The LA retractor typically has a more vertical entry into the chest. The left arm port can sometimes be difficult to pass through in muscular patients or those with small rib spaces, requiring careful and controlled use of force. The left arm needs to be far enough from the shoulder to avoid conflict, but too far forward is unhelpful for later angles required for valve repair. Using the third ICS instead of the second helps with this. The introducers are left in place temporarily for the right and left arm ports, as they often get caught under the soft tissue retractor and are easier to manipulate while these are still in place.
	To place the vent we first enter the chest just posterior to the main incision in the fourth ICS using tonsillectomy forceps. This location allows for easy access and ensures that we can easily manage any potential bleeding at the end of the case.
	The basket vent is then passed inside in a fashion similar to surgical chest drain insertion, with the tip covered by a length of 16Fr urinary catheter to prevent any fat or debris from lodging inside. Next we place an Alexis soft tissue retractor (Applied Medical, California, USA) within the working port, after passing the basket vent through its middle, and thereafter keeping the vent tucked under one side of it for safe storage until needed later in the case.
	Finally, we manipulate the ports if any are caught within the retractor and then remove the introducers.
	We administer heparin during port placement so that we are ready for cannulation as soon as we reach this point and avoid any unnecessary delays.

Our approach to positioning and port placement has evolved through iterative refinements across more than 2400 robotic mitral valve repairs. Over time, our standard incisions have shifted progressively more laterally, resulting in improved exposure. The described current approach is reproducible and consistently offers optimal visualization. While 5 patients in our initial cohort of 1000 robotic cases required conversion to conventional incisions due to inadequate exposure, no conversions were necessary after chest incision for any reason in our most recent reported series \[1,2\]. Nonetheless, conversion should not be delayed if deemed necessary, as patient safety is paramount.

Robotic mitral valve repair can and should be performed with equivalent repair rates, durability and safety profile to sternotomy, with the added benefit of enhanced post-operative recovery \[1–6\]. The key lies in the appropriate selection of candidates, particularly during the early phase of a programme. Identifying risk factors better managed with sternotomy access is critical to avoiding complications.

To this end, we developed a deliberately conservative screening algorithm based on echocardiography and CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis including the femoral vessels \[2\]. Echo findings favouring a sternal approach include left ventricular ejection fraction of <50%, pulmonary artery systolic pressure of >60 mmHg, more than mild aortic regurgitation, and significant mitral annular calcification. Relevant CT findings relate primarily to peripheral cannulation for cardiopulmonary bypass, including more than mild aortoiliac atherosclerosis and femoral artery diameter of <7 mm, as well as chest wall abnormalities such as severe pectus excavatum.

The routine use of preoperative CT has resulted in a 75% reduction in the stroke rate from our earliest cases, when it was not used regularly, down to 0.5% \[2\]. The overall mortality rate for robotic mitral repair at our institution is 0.04%.

Port site infections are exceedingly rare \[1\]. Re-exploration for bleeding has declined from 2.5% to 1.3% over time and can almost always be addressed from the right chest \[1,2\]. While not a frequent issue in our experience, occult hepatic bleeding can also occur, with a reported 3.5% incidence during the recent launch of a robotic programme in Europe \[7\]. Using the tips noted within the steps above should help to mitigate this risk.

Experience and case volume remain critical determinants of success. Approximately 40 cumulative cases per centre are required to cross the national mean for mortality and morbidity rates \[8\]. However, the learning curve can be accelerated through the pursuit of dedicated robotic fellowships, team-based simulation, team visits to high-volume centres, and structured mentorship. Detailed consensus recommendations have been published and offer valuable guidance for robotic cardiac surgical training and program development \[9\].

In expert hands, robotic mitral valve surgery is associated with a lower need for blood transfusion, shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, reduced length of stay in ICU and in hospital, fewer readmissions, and an earlier return to work and normal life \[2,3,8,10\]. Moreover, mitral valve repair is possible in nearly all cases \[1,2\], with excellent long-term durability comparable to that of conventional approaches \[4–6,10\].
