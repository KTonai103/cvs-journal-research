Robotic mitral repair is often associated with longer ischaemic and cardiopulmonary bypass times, particularly early in the learning curve. We demonstrate a semi-continuous, three-suture technique for robotic annuloplasty that retains the mechanical principles of traditional interrupted sutures while leveraging the advantages of robotic precision and exposure. The use of pre-knotted sutures minimizes intra-cardiac knot tying, further enhancing procedural efficiency.

Annuloplasty is essential in mitral valve repair, reinforcing the annulus to restore and maintain optimal geometry and improving long-term durability \[1\]. Adapting traditional open annuloplasty techniques to robotic mitral valve repair requires thoughtful modifications to preserve effectiveness while enhancing efficiency. Although interrupted sutures remain the standard in open repair, they can be time-consuming and technically demanding in robotic cases, even with automated suture fasteners.

Some robotic techniques simplify annuloplasty by passing sutures around, rather than through, the annuloplasty band, potentially compromising the remodelling effect. Our approach adapts the principles of interrupted suturing into a semi-continuous three-suture technique in which each bite passes sequentially through the annulus and the flexible band, thereby preserving true annular remodelling \[2\].

This method streamlines band placement while maintaining the biomechanical goals of annuloplasty. The robotic platform’s enhanced visualization and instrument articulation enables precise suture placement and optimal exposure, resulting in an efficient, reproducible technique that closely mirrors the benefits of traditional open annuloplasty.

- 1 - Suture #1 (0:16)
	The flexible annuloplasty band is taken off the holder and brought inside the left atrium. A semi-continuous technique using three sutures is used for robotic cases.
	The first suture is placed at the medial trigone, initially passing the needle down through the annuloplasty band one away from the end. A backhand stitch is next taken anteriorly, which is then brought back up through the band at the very end, before reversing and continuing clockwise around the annulus.
	Each stitch goes down through the annuloplasty band from atrial to ventricular aspect, through the annulus, back up through the band and is then pulled up and cinched down.
	This technique takes full advantage of the dexterity of the robotic arms while achieving the same result as an open or interrupted technique.
	The three sutures are pre-knotted with eight knots at the end to minimize the number of knots that need to be tied with the robot, thus improving efficiency; the first is tied at 11cm, the second at 10cm and the last at 8cm, and each is left with a 1.5cm tail.
	A flexible band is key for this technique, and our preference is to use a Duran annuloplasty band (Medtronic, Minnesota, USA).
	The first stitch is continued until approximately the middle of P2, where the second stitch will be started. It is important to keep track of how much annulus and band are remaining in order to end in the appropriate position at the left trigone.
	Each new downward bite on the annuloplasty band and the annulus enter in close proximity to where the previous bite came out.
	It is important to hold the annuloplasty band close to where the needle is being passed through to provide counter-traction, particularly after a number of stitches have already been taken and the needle begins to become blunt.
	One advantage of the robot is being able to reposition the left atrial retractor at any point during the procedure to optimize exposure.
	Gentle traction on the valve repair sutures can help to expose the annulus, and countertraction is also provided by the bedside assistant using the sucker to push the atrial wall away.
- 2 - Suture #2 (3:56)
	After the second stitch goes down through the band, is taken through the annulus, and back up through the band, the first stitch is tightened again. It is then tied to the tail of the second suture.
	The needle and suture tails are handed back to the bedside assistant and then the second suture is continued in a similar fashion clockwise towards the left trigone. This stitch completes the majority of the rest of the annuloplasty until there is just one bite remaining.
	The left arm can be used to push down the atrium to enhance visualization. It is particularly important to stick close to the annulus in this region (from approximately the 7 o’clock position until the end) as this is where the circumflex coronary artery is most at risk.
	Retracting the band posteriorly can also aid with exposure.
	After each stitch the band is cinched down to ensure a good annuloplasty.
	Exposure is optimized whenever needed with a slight readjustment of the left atrial retractor, as well as the camera, both of which are under the direct control of the operating surgeon.
	The left arm plays an important part at this stage, retracting the band posteriorly, then pushing down on the atrium for exposure, and finally teasing the needle through at the level of the annulus.
- 3 - Suture #3 (7:07)
	The third stitch enters the band at the very end. It is taken as a backhand stitch anticlockwise through the trigone. After it is brought back up through the valve and tightened, the suture is cut. The end of the second stitch is tightened up and then tied to the third stitch. After seven secure “lucky” knots, the tails are cut and the annuloplasty is complete.

Annuloplasty is crucial for the long-term durability of mitral valve repair, with increased rates of recurrent mitral regurgitation observed when it is omitted \[3,4\]. Accordingly, annuloplasty is performed in 100% of patients undergoing robotic mitral valve repair at our institution \[5,6\]. While a variety of rings and bands with differing rigidity are available, we prefer partial posterior flexible bands as dilatation occurs predominantly in the posterior annulus and long-term durability is comparable across annuloplasty types \[5,7–9\].

Complete rings require additional sutures in the anterior annulus, increasing the small but important risk of aortic valve injury, and have also been identified as a risk factor for functional mitral stenosis \[10,11\]. Systolic anterior motion (SAM) is more likely with complete rings, particularly when undersized. For degenerative disease, we err toward larger bands and utilize anterior leaflet surface area rather than inter-trigonal distance when sizing \[7\].

Flexible bands are especially advantageous in robotic surgery as they are easy to handle and allow repeated needle passage without significant needle dulling, an important consideration for the semi-continuous technique described. The running annuloplasty was initially developed to reduce operating times in robotic mitral valve repair and its introduction was associated with a 19-minute reduction in median aortic occlusion time \[2\].

Each bite must be placed into the annulus rather than the atrium to minimize the risk of late dehiscence (most commonly seen with rigid rings) and injury to the circumflex artery \[12,13\]. This is particularly important in those with left or co-dominant systems, where the circumflex coronary artery lies anatomically closer to the mitral annulus \[13\].

Although we use a partial band in almost all cases, there remains a risk of aortic valve injury with the final suture at the lateral trigone \[10\]. Awareness of the needle’s depth and angle is critical at this step, and routine assessment for new or worsened aortic regurgitation should form part of every intra-operative transoesophageal echo evaluation prior to separation from bypass.

In summary, this semi-continuous three-suture annuloplasty technique combines the biomechanical advantages of traditional interrupted sutures with the efficiency and precision of robotic technology, significantly reducing operating times while supporting long-term repair durability.
