Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy is a disease of the heart in which the patient develops left ventricular outflow tract obstruction due to the interaction of the mitral valve and the intraventricular septum. Although septal myectomy remains the gold standard treatment for hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, several other approaches have been described in the literature, such as a transaortic, transapical or transmitral approach via a sternotomy. All of these approaches have been shown to produce reliable reduction in left ventricular outflow tract gradients. Robotic-assisted cardiac surgery has recently become a safe and effective alternative approach to a sternotomy for several intracardiac procedures, especially mitral valve repair and, in experienced centres, septal myectomy.

The natural history of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) leads to left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction \[1\] due to the interaction of the mitral valve and the interventricular septum and to mitral valve regurgitation due to systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior mitral leaflet, which can result in sudden cardiac death or progressive heart failure. Surgical myectomy remains the gold standard treatment of HOCM \[2,3\]. Recently, a robotic approach for several intracardiac procedures \[4\], especially mitral valve repair, has proved to be a reproducible approach with an excellent safety profile and longitudinal outcomes \[5\]. We describe a robotic endoscopic transmitral septal myectomy with concomitant mitral valve repair in a 54-year-old patient.

- 1 - Patient presentation (0:12)
	We present a symptomatic 54-year-old woman with HOCM and severe mitral regurgitation (MR). A transthoracic echocardiogram showed asymmetrical interventricular septal hypertrophy of 2.46 cm with severe MR due to intrinsic mitral disease (annulus dilatation and posterior leaflet prolapse) and SAM contributing to the obstruction of the LVOT and LVOT, with mean and maximum gradients of 55 mmHg and 123 mmHg, respectively (Figure 1).

- 2 - Patient position and port placement (0:53)
	The patient gave informed consent and was taken to the operating room, positioned supine with a roll under the right side. The right shoulder was deflected posteriorly, and the right arm was placed at the level of the bed (Figure 2a).
	**Figure 2a**
	![](https://mmcts.org/media/3c4e9509-f6d7-40f2-bbf8-56862430b249)
	The bedside assistant and the scrub nurse were located to the right of the patient while the robot came in from the left side and docked to the right chest ports in the standard configuration for robotic mitral valve repair. Five ports were placed: (i) a working port (8 mm) with the XX small Alexis (soft tissue retractor) in the 4th intercostal space medial to the anterior axillary line; (ii) a camera port (8 mm) was placed medially to the working port for a camera in the 4th intercostal space; (iii+iv) two ports (8 mm) were placed in the 2nd and the 6th intercostal spaces, in the same line with the working port; (v) the third arm (left atrial retractor) port was placed medially to the mid-clavicular line in the 5th intercostal space (Figure 2b).
	**Figure 2b**
	![](https://mmcts.org/media/a7871632-992c-40e1-8788-510eaf31b281) ***Figure 2.** (a) Set-up for a robotic interventricular septal resection. The patient is positioned with the right hemithorax elevated 30° and the right arm tucked by the patient’s side. (b) Placement of the robotic instrument arm trocar. Five port sites technique: (1) An assistant port (12 mm) with an XX small Alexis wound protector/retractor in the fourth intercostal space was placed medial to the anterior axillary line. (2) A camera port (8 mm) was placed medial to the working port for the camera. (3, 4) Two working ports (8 mm) were placed in the second and the sixth intercostal spaces along the line with the assistant port. (5) The third (retractor) port was placed medially to and one intercostal space below the camera port.*
	General endotracheal anaesthesia with a single lumen tube was initiated. The femoral artery and vein were accessed through a 2-cm incision in the right groin. We prefer to cannulate the femoral artery above the inguinal ligament where the artery is bigger and to minimize the risk of limb ischaemia by preserving collateral perfusion from its branches. Once heparin was given, and under transoesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) guidance to assure proper positioning of the cannulas and the guidewires, the catheter in the right internal jugular vein originally placed by the anaesthesia team was replaced with a superior vena cava cannula, a long right femoral venous cannula positioned with the tip just below the vena cava cannula and a standard right femoral arterial cannula.
- 3 - Intrathoracic set-up (1:42)
	With adequate activating clotting time, cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated, and the ventilator circuit was disconnected, after which the da Vinci Xi robotic system (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) was docked. Under CO2 insufflation, a diaphragmatic retraction stitch using 3-0 Prolene was placed on the anterior-medial, mid-medial and posterior-medial segments of the central tendon of the diaphragm and brought out anterolaterally from the chest wall for retraction and improved exposure of the pericardium/right atrium. The pericardium was then opened longitudinally anterior to the phrenic nerve. Two pericardial retraction sutures were placed and brought out posterior-lateral to the working port. The oblique sinus was opened, Sondergaard’s groove was developed, and the plane between the aorta and the right PA was developed. The procedure can be performed with either endoaortic balloon occlusion or a transthoracic Chitwood clamp. Our standard approach for mitral valve surgery is the endoaortic balloon; however, to fully decompress the aortic root and obtain a clearer view of the interventricular septum, we used the transthoracic Chitwood clamp with antegrade and retrograde cardioplegia. The anaesthesia team placed a retrograde cannula into the coronary sinus through the right internal jugular with TEE guidance. The transthoracic Chitwood clamp was brought in through the second intercostal space in the midaxillary line. The aorta was cross-clamped in the mid ascending region after dissection of the aortopulmonary window. A long percutaneous 14 G cardioplegia catheter was placed in the second intercostal space, proximal to the cross-clamp. Antegrade del Nido crystalloid cardioplegia was administered in the root to achieve a flaccid diastolic arrest; subsequent cardioplegia doses were given retrogradely throughout the procedure.
- 4 - Transmitral myectomy (3:19)
	CO2 was used to flood the field at a flow of 2 L/min. A generous left atriotomy was made along Sondergaard’s groove; the robotic dynamic atrial retractor was positioned to expose the mitral valve. With the aid of the robotic suction/irrigation system, the mitral valve was studied, and the leaflet coaptation was marked by measuring the length of the long axis of the A2 and P2 segments. The mitral valve’s annulus was severely dilated due to intrinsic posterior leaflet disease. The plan was made to proceed by working on the myectomy portion and to address the mitral valve pathology on the way out. A generous incision was made in the anterior leaflet from the medial trigone to the lateral trigone, leaving a 2-mm cuff of the anterior leaflet to the annulus to aid with the repair (Figure 3). The atrial retractor was then positioned to expose the interventricular septum through this incision.
	![](https://mmcts.org/media/d41a74dd-36c4-4cab-bb35-9632a6579306)
	***Figure 3.** (a) Measuring the distance from the medial to the lateral trigone to aid with closure planning. (b) A generous incision was made in the anterior leaflet from the medial trigone and lateral trigone using Potts scissors, leaving a 2-mm cuff of the anterior leaflet to the annulus to aid with the repair. (c) Exposure of the interventricular septum and aortic valve through the mobilized mitral valve anterior leaflet.*
	The transmitral septal myectomy was performed by incising the thickened septum extending from the 12 o’clock position anticlockwise to the 9 o’clock position (Figure 4a).
	![](https://mmcts.org/media/9d0631c7-d699-4f17-8555-f789546aa3a9)
	***Figure 4.** (a) Resection of the septal muscle. The transmitral septal myectomy was performed by incising the thickened septum, extending from the right to the nadir of the right coronary cusp anticlockwise toward the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve. The thickness of the cut was based on the preoperative transoesophageal echocardiographic septum thickness. (b) Myectomy extended to midventricular septum. (c) The trabeculation muscle opposite the papillary muscles was excised.*
	The thickness of the cut was based on the preoperative thickness of the TEE septum. Muscle resection was extended to the midventricular level, opposite to the papillary muscle to relieve any midventricular obstruction (Figure 4b, 4c). Using the high-definition 3-dimensional robotic camera visualization (either 30° or 0° angle) can facilitate identifying areas with incomplete myectomy and asymmetrical septum thickness. Thickened secondary chordae tendinea to the anterior leaflet are also visualized and transected if appropriate. The perfusion team briefly filled the right ventricle to evaluate septum thickness and to check for a ventricular septal defect.
- 5 - Mitral valve repair and completion of the procedure (5:16)
	Upon completion of the myectomy, the anterior leaflet was reconstructed with a 0.8-mm wide ultrathin bovine pericardium strip using running 4-0 Prolene suture. Once completed, the mitral valve was then checked with the pressurized saline test, in which the leaflet coaptation level was marked and any leak was noted. In this patient, two sets of neochordae (CV-4 polytetrafluoroethylene) were attached to the posterior leaflet to move the coaptation level more posteriorly. In addition, a mitral annuloplasty was performed with a 34-mm semi-rigid partial annuloplasty ring (Simulus, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). It was sutured from the trigone to the trigone along the posterior annulus using a continuous running mattress stitch with 3-0 Prolene suture (Figure 5).
	![](https://mmcts.org/media/7bee1133-51b5-4df8-a996-bdbb51e00a6a) ***Figure 5.** (a) Following completion of the myectomy, the anterior leaflet was reconstructed with a 0.8-mm wide ultrathin bovine pericardium strip using running 4-0 Prolene. (b) Mitral valve repair was performed with two sets of neochords and annuloplasty using a 34 semi-rigid partial annuloplasty ring (Simulus, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). \[c\] Transoesophageal echocardiogram shows left ventricular outlet tract “pre- versus post-” septal myectomy mean gradients.*
	Once the mitral valve was determined to be competent by the final saline test, with no evidence of leak and a low and adequate leaflet coaptation level, the left atriotomy was closed with running 4-0 Prolene. Once the heart was de-aired, the angiocath was removed, and the site was repaired with pledgeted 4-0 Prolene, after which the cross-clamp was removed. Atrial and ventricular pacing wires were placed and brought out through the left atrial retractor port site. A 24 Fr channel drain was placed through the left atrial retractor port site and into the pericardial space through the oblique sinus. The pericardium was closed loosely with a V-Loc suture (Covidien, New Haven, CT, USA). The robot was undocked, and ventilation was resumed. Cardiopulmonary bypass was then discontinued, and both venous cannulas from the right internal jugular vein and right common femoral vein were removed. Protamine was given. The right common femoral artery cannula was then removed. Once haemostasis was confirmed, all port sites were closed.
- 6 - Postoperative transoesophageal echocardiogram (6:44)
	The postoperative TEE showed no evidence of MR or LVOT obstruction. The interventricular septum was thinner, and the mean gradient improved from 55 mmHg preoperatively to 5 mmHg postoperatively. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 and was doing well with complete resolution of symptoms at the 3-month follow-up visit.

We present a less-invasive approach for septal myectomy and mitral valve repair compared to the traditional transaortic approach that requires a sternotomy. The postoperative echocardiogram demonstrated the successful results of this approach.

Transatrial septal myectomy was originally introduced by Lillehei and Levy in 1963 for two young patients with subaortic stenosis via a right posterolateral thoracotomy \[5\]. This approach provides excellent exposure of the interior of the left ventricle and especially the low ventricular septum and minimizes the risk of aortic valve injury. The disadvantage of this approach is the need for a thoracotomy, and it is associated with severe postoperative pain, pulmonary complications, longer hospital lengths of stay and prolonged recovery, which do not differ from (and may be more severe than) a sternotomy approach \[6\].

Robotic cardiac surgery provides a minimally invasive, endoscopic approach for the management of intracardiac pathologies. It has been proven to be a safe and effective approach in mitral valve surgery, aortic valve surgery, tricuspid valve surgery, coronary revascularization \[7\], arrhythmia surgery, congenital cardiac operations and cardiac tumour resections.

Robotic endoscopic transmitral septal myectomy was originally reported by Dr Randy Chitwood and colleagues in 2012 as a less-invasive approach for transmitral septal myectomy \[8\]. The approach allowed for an adequate interventricular septal resection and a significant decrease in aortic outflow tract gradients with no residual SAM. In the original paper, the detached anterior mitral leaflet was treated with a primary repair. We, however, used a bovine pericardium ultrathin patch in our patient, given that the anterior leaflet was short, leading to a potentially high leaflet coaptation level. We have performed this procedure with primary repair of the anterior leaflet in patients with a tall anterior leaflet, which helps prevent SAM. The disadvantage of a primary repair is the potential for significant shortening of the anterior leaflet, which can lead to SAM and/or MR. This approach endorses the importance of the preoperative echocardiogram, the intraoperative measurement of the anterior leaflet length and the demarcation of the coaptation level prior to incising the anterior leaflet. We used two sets of neochords; 4-0 polytetrafluoroethylene sutures were placed to bring down the posterior leaflet and move the leaflet coaptation level posteriorly. Because the annulus was moderately dilated, it was reinforced by implanting a semi-rigid annuloplasty band to preserve the dynamic mitral coaptation.

Recurrent LVOT obstruction causing symptoms is uncommon after successful myectomy; however, a common cause is midventricular obstruction due to inadequate myectomy during the first operation \[9\]. Using the robotic transmitral approach provides a wide view of the septum from the subaortic level to the midventricular level, which allows one to extend and lengthen the myectomy toward the apex of the heart, thereby mobilizing the anomalous papillary muscle \[10\] and/or shaving the hypertrophied papillary muscle to alleviate all obstructions, and facilitates any mitral valve repair techniques that may be required \[11\].

The robotic exposure allows visualization of the mitral leaflets and their primary and secondary chords. Resection of the secondary chordae has recently been shown to aid with moving the mitral valve apparatus posteriorly and relieving the LVOT obstruction \[12\]. We rely on secondary chordae resection using this approach for selected patients with significant LVOT obstruction, mostly from severe SAM, and where the septum may be only mildly thickened.

The robotic platform adds a 3-dimensional, 10× magnified high-definition wide view of the entire LVOT to delineate precisely the borders of the interventricular septum \[8,13\]. It helps to identify the aortic valve cusps, membranous septum, papillary muscles and ventricular cavity. The ergonomic robotic instruments help in excising the septum precisely in very tight quadrants. We prefer the use of the robotic micro-Potts scissors, which are equipped with a ruler for accurate measurement of the depth of the resection.

This approach avoids the potential complications of the traditional open septal myectomy approach via a sternotomy, which include a long recovery period, aortotomy site bleeding, aortic cusp damage \[14\], perforation or laceration of the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve, damage to the conducting system and lastly but more importantly, an inadequate septal myectomy due to poor visualization.

We previously published the outcomes of >1000 patients who underwent robotic endoscopic left- and right-sided on- and off-pump cardiac operations with favourable results. Compared to a traditional sternotomy approach, we believe that a robotic endoscopic procedure results in less postoperative pain, superior healing, a better cosmetic result, less morbidity, fewer blood transfusions, shorter stays in the intensive care unit and in the hospital, and quicker recovery overall \[15\].

In conclusion, robotic transmitral septal myectomy is a safe, effective alternative approach to the standard open septal myectomy via a sternotomy. The magnified 3-dimensional high-definition robotic camera and ergonomic robotic instruments help to precisely excise the septum without leaving any septal asymmetry and to avoid damaging adjacent structures. Candidates for this approach are symptomatic patients in need of a septal myectomy for a hypertrophic septum with or without a mitral valve component. A team experienced in intracardiac robotic cardiac surgery (e.g. mitral valve repair) and comfortable with complex robotic skills is necessary for a successful outcome using this approach.

**Case Report Follow-up**

The patient returned to the clinic for the 3-month follow-up with complete resolution of symptoms. The repeat transthoracic echocardiogram showed no residual MR and/or SAM.
