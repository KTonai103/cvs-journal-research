Systolic anterior motion remains a recognized complication of mitral valve repair, for which sliding valvuloplasty was originally developed as a preventive strategy. This technique is used in patients with a broad area of posterior leaflet prolapse and excessive leaflet height. We demonstrate our modification of the classic sliding plasty, including further adaptations to enable its reliable and efficient application via a minimally invasive robotic approach.

Patients with excess posterior leaflet height are at a particular risk of developing systolic anterior motion (SAM) following mitral valve repair. Sliding valvuloplasty was developed to mitigate this risk and is typically indicated when the posterior leaflet height exceeds 15 mm. A modification of the classic sliding plasty is performed in both open and robotic cases at our institution, with additional suture adaptations employed to facilitate its reliable and efficient application in the robotic setting.

- 1 - Introduction and valve assessment (0:17)
	Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) demonstrates extensive P2 prolapse with severe anteriorly directed mitral regurgitation.
	Intraoperative findings are similar with a normal anterior leaflet and a broad prolapsing P2 and with particular note taken of tall P1 and P3 segments.
- 2 - Quadrangular resection (0:39)
	A quadrangular resection of the affected portion is made while identifying the adjacent normal chords and ensuring their preservation. Given the extent of prolapse, a large amount of leaflet tissue is necessarily removed.
- 3 - Bidirectional sliding plasty and leaflet repair (1:14)
	To reapproximate the leaflet edges without tension as well as to reduce the height of the adjacent segments to prevent postoperative SAM, a bidirectional sliding plasty is performed.
	The base of the leaflet is incised parallel to the annulus and extended in both directions, first towards P3 and then towards P1. Secondary chords are divided to mobilize the leaflet but care is taken to preserve all primary chords.
	The first suture is taken part way up the leaflet towards the free edge. Where this suture is positioned determines the new height of the leaflet at that point. The leaflet is then pulled across and attached to the annulus. A similar stitch is taken on the other side, again noting that where the stitch is taken on the leaflet will determine the height of the new leaflet. Finally, the free edge of the leaflet is approximated with a simple interrupted stitch that positions the leaflet to be sewn back together.
	We typically begin at either edge and work towards the middle of the repair. However, in this instance, the leaflet seemed to be too tight and to open a cleft between P2 and P3, so we opted to remove the suture and start from the centre.
	In open cases, a running suture is used for this portion of the repair, but it is difficult to maintain adequate tension on the suture while using the robot so we utilize interrupted sutures instead.
	The depth of bite into the leaflet body is varied depending on how much shortening of the leaflet height is required.
	In this case we opted to add an annular plication suture to the area that appeared under tension earlier.
	After the medial portion is completed, the lateral portion is addressed in a similar fashion, this time doing so in the more usual way by starting at the outer edge and working towards the midline. Again, interrupted sutures are used rather than running the full length in order to ensure adequate closure.
	The next step is to close the leaflet body itself, which is performed in a fashion similar to that of a triangular resection closure. The initially placed suture at the free edge is used to gently lift up and expose the leaflet as well as to provide countertraction.
	Additional sutures are taken as necessary to eliminate residual defects that could cause minor regurgitation. Often, we secure both sides of the repair to provide complete reassurance.
- 4 - Annuloplasty and passive testing (7:55)
	The annuloplasty is performed next, in this case taking care to follow the neoannulus across the annular plication. We use a semi-continuous running technique for robotic cases, which we demonstrated in full in a separate video \[1\].
	Passive testing demonstrates a small residual leak with a suggestion of a small defect at the base of the leaflet that is reinforced once again with Prolene.
- 5 - Left atrial closure, removal of cross-clamp and TOE (9:14)
	The left atrium is closed in the usual fashion, and the aortic cross-clamp is removed. The result on TOE is excellent with no mitral regurgitation, no SAM and a mean gradient of 2 mmHg.
	In summary, a sliding valvuloplasty is a good technique when a significant volume of tissue has been resected and the adjacent leaflet segments are tall.

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, our group and others began to recognize left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction as a specific complication of mitral valve repair that occurred in approximately 4%–9% of cases overall \[2–5\]. Detailed echocardiographic and anatomical analyses led Carpentier to propose that the mechanism involved overlap between the left ventricular functional inflow and outflow compartments, along with a mismatch in leaflet volume, causing the posterior leaflet to close ahead of the anterior leaflet \[5–7\]. In response, he developed the sliding leaflet repair technique, or sliding plasty \[6\].

By reducing the posterior leaflet surface relative to the ventricular cavity and preserving anterior leaflet predominance to initiate systolic closure, this approach was felt to fully address the pathophysiologic basis of SAM \[6,7\]. From 1988 to 1991, a total of 82 patients at high risk for SAM underwent mitral repair with the sliding leaflet technique at his institution \[7\]. Only two patients developed postoperative LVOT gradients, both of which were mild (<20 mmHg) —a marked contrast to an earlier series from the same group reporting a 14% incidence of SAM in patients with predisposing anatomical factors \[5,7\].

We later described a modification of the classic sliding plasty, in which we avoid triangular resection at the leaflet base \[8\]. Instead, the entire leaflet is preserved to provide additional support, and suture depth is varied to achieve the desired degree of shortening \[8\]. This modification improves operative efficiency and also facilitates revision, should inadequate coaptation be encountered. In our series of 230 patients, all underwent successful valve repair without any instances of SAM \[8\].

In our early robotic experience, we observed a higher incidence of SAM requiring reoperation with resectional techniques (0.98%) compared to those treated with neochords (0%) \[9\]. However, we believe that resection provides superior long-term durability in our hands, and we now more liberally employ sliding plasty and folding plasty in robotic cases than we did at the outset of our program, as well as hybrid repairs with the addition of neochords \[10\]. The transition from a running suture to multiple interrupted sutures has significantly improved our comfort with, and the reproducibility of, robotic sliding plasty.

Additional strategies to minimize SAM include the use of larger annuloplasty sizes and posterior bands rather than complete rings \[11,12\]. We routinely use flexible partial annuloplasty bands in our practice, regardless of the risk of SAM.

Independent predictors of SAM include a left ventricular end-diastolic diameter <45 mm, coaptation-septum distance <25 mm, basal septal thickness >15 mm, aorto-mitral angle <120° and a posterior leaflet height >15 mm \[11\]. Despite, or perhaps because of, improved screening and heightened awareness, SAM continues to occur in 4%–8% of modern series, even at high-volume centres \[11,12\].

Fortunately, SAM is typically amenable to medical management, which includes afterload augmentation, preload optimization, heart rate reduction and the avoidance of inotropes \[13\]. Over 85% of cases identified in the operating room respond to these conservative measures when appropriate surgical strategies have been deployed \[12,13\]. For those with persistent ≥2+ mitral regurgitation or an LVOT gradient >50 mmHg, a second pump run to address the problem surgically is usually warranted, taking into account patient age and comorbidities.

Most commonly in our practice, we now add short posterior neochords, which have shown favourable outcomes at other institutions as well \[13\]. Other strategies include removal or re-implantation of a larger annuloplasty device, other posterior leaflet shortening techniques, edge-to-edge repair or mitral valve replacement \[12,14,15\] Notably, most mitral reoperations for SAM at our institution, which includes many outside referrals, were amenable to re-repair, suggesting that many could have been avoided if addressed at the index operation \[16\].

Coronary anatomy must also be considered when planning the repair strategy. Injury to the circumflex coronary artery is a recognized risk of sliding plasty, particularly in patients with left dominant circulation \[17\].

Although we strongly favour resectional techniques overall, adequate residual leaflet tissue is essential to bridge the gap. We generally avoid annular plication in cases with elevated risk of SAM, as this displaces the line of coaptation anteriorly. However, in select cases such as the one presented, plication remains a useful adjunct to relieve excessive leaflet tension that might otherwise predispose to leaflet dehiscence.

Carpentier historically recommended valve replacement when more than one-third of the posterior leaflet was affected by ruptured chordae \[18\]. Today, these cases are often repairable and best served using neochords, but appropriate case selection and intraoperative judgement remain paramount.

Ultimately the best treatment for SAM is its avoidance, and for patients with broad posterior leaflet prolapse and tall adjacent leaflet segments, sliding valvuloplasty remains an indispensable technique with excellent long-term results \[19\]. With the aforementioned modifications, sliding valvuloplasty can also be performed reliably and reproducibly via a minimally invasive robotic approach.
