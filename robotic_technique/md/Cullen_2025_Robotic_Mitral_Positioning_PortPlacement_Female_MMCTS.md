There are several important differences when positioning and placing ports for robotic mitral surgery in female patients. Herein we demonstrate our technique while highlighting some important tips and tricks. This approach almost universally results in a perfect view, as well as a scar that is cosmetically pleasing.

Despite a higher incidence of mitral valve prolapse, female patients are under-represented in mitral surgery, particularly robotic mitral repair \[1–5\]. Women are referred less frequently and later in the disease course, contributing to worse outcomes compared with males \[6\]. These outcomes may in turn reinforce hesitancy to refer or operate. Current surgical referral guidelines, derived largely from male-predominant cohorts, fail to fully account for sex-based anatomical and physiological differences, even when indexed to body surface area (BSA) \[7\].

While delayed referral and more advanced disease partly explain this disparity, anatomical considerations in female patients, such as chest wall dimensions, vessel size and how best to position the breast, also significantly influence the technical aspects of robotic surgery. Effective management of these factors ensures both safety and optimized visualization, thus improving outcomes.

Herein we provide a practical overview of patient positioning and port site planning for robotic mitral repair in female patients, drawing from high-volume institutional experience. Our aim is to help surgeons achieve consistent results, increase procedural confidence and broaden the adoption of robotic techniques across both sexes.

- 1 - Marking and positioning (0:16)
	Precise patient marking is a critical first step to ensure optimal exposure. It is especially important to mark the midline perfectly in female patients, in case conversion to sternotomy is later required, as this will be retracted significantly off-centre, rendering surface landmarks unreliable. This should be done while the patient is still supine, with arms tucked by their side. The fourth intercostal space (ICS) is identified and marked by counting down from the manubriosternal junction, followed by the inferior and lateral border of the right breast.
	The breast is then retracted superiorly and towards the midline, which allows the position of the left atrial (LA) retractor port in the inframammary crease over the fourth intercostal space to be identified and marked. This is the most reliable and helpful mark for the rest of the case so it is important to get it right.
	Finally, we mark the groin. The anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and pubic tubercle are marked and joined with a line, followed by the groin crease, and then a vertical line along the femoral pulse. The incision for the femoral vessels is about 1/2 to 2/3 of the way from the groin crease to the ASIS/pubic tubercle line. If there are any concerns regarding either a high bifurcation or atherosclerosis then an ultrasound is performed, but in most cases marking based on surface landmarks is sufficient. Ultrasound can also be helpful in the case of “borderline” vessel size on preoperative imaging.
	The patient is lifted towards the right side of the bed and the chest is elevated with a rolled towel placed vertically from just underneath the tip of the scapula. A custom-made metal sled is inserted under the mattress and the arm is supported in a foam sling, ensuring it is lying in a good position with adequate exposure of the axilla. Additional foam is placed under the right shoulder to support it.
	The anterior axillary line is then marked between the deltopectoral groove and the ASIS. Importantly, this mark should be made with the breast retracted towards the left shoulder. The working incision will be lateral to the breast and is usually a more vertical incision than in male patients. The diaphragm retraction suture is in front of the anterior axillary line, and the right arm port is posterior to it, in the sixth or seventh ICS depending on the size of the rib spaces. The left arm port is often in the second ICS in females to leave space between it and the working port incision, and we then mark the location of the basket vent and the two pericardial retraction sutures posteriorly.
- 2 - Draping (1:51)
	After sterile preparation, we lay a sterile drape along the patient. This is to facilitate strapping the legs in position to prevent the potential for falling off the table, a risk due to the steep leftward angulation employed during the robotic component of the case.
	We are careful to drape in the right internal jugular (RIJ) vein cannula, which was placed by the anaesthesiology team during insertion of their routine lines. This is used to cannulate the superior vena cava (SVC) in patients with BSA approaching 2 m2 or above, as well as those undergoing tricuspid repair.
	A strip of Ioban (Solventum, Minnesota, USA) is used to retract the breast. We start by covering the working incision and retracting such that the previously placed marking of the anterior axillary line becomes straight. One of the circulating team then grabs the yellow sticky portion of the Ioban and pulls in the same direction. It is phenomenally stretchy but also strong, and it can be stuck securely to the bottom of the operating table to keep the breast retracted for the entirety of the case.
	Another strip of Ioban is placed posterolaterally from bottom to top, ensuring the marks for the two pericardial retraction sutures are incorporated, and the axilla is accessible, followed by a third to cover the groin, and a fourth to cover the rest of the exposed skin. The main surgical drape is then placed, again being careful to leave the axilla and surgical marks exposed, as well as to ensure easy access to the previously placed RIJ cannula.
	Finally, the electrocautery, vent and CO2 tubing is passed up towards the patient’s right shoulder underneath the manifold for the cardiopulmonary bypass lines, along with a length of tubing, which will connect the RIJ cannula to the bypass circuit.
- 3 - Femoral exposure (3:05)
	A 3 cm femoral incision is made and electrocautery used to dissect down to and expose the front of the common femoral artery and vein.
	Meticulous haemostasis is ensured, and we also take note of any large lymph nodes, which we will tie off at the end of the case to minimize the risk of seroma formation.
	We always expose the femoral artery before proceeding to the chest, as there have been occasions where the femoral artery goes into spasm just from exposure, which we feel renders it unsafe for cannulation and cardiopulmonary bypass. Although we could attempt to pharmacologically dilate the visible portion of the vessel, our concern is that if there is unrecognized spasm proximal to the cannula then we may be inadvertently under-perfusing the body and brain for the duration of the procedure. This is more common in female patients and usually encountered in patients who have already been identified as having “borderline” (but not prohibitive) vessel size on preoperative computed tomography (CT).
- 4 - Working port (3:25)
	A 4 cm incision is then made in the chest lateral to the breast where previously marked, again using deliberate electrocautery to ensure meticulous haemostasis. In this location, pectoralis major is often not encountered and the first muscle seen is the serratus anterior, which is divided along and between its fibres. The retractor is manipulated as necessary to provide exposure. The rib is identified underneath the serratus, and the intercostal muscles are slowly divided.
	The lungs are disconnected fully and the pleural space is entered bluntly. The lung usually deflates well but may require some active suction to be applied by the anaesthesiology team, or occasionally tube repositioning may be necessary, after which the pleura is opened further. In this case the lung remains inflated after the pleura is opened so caution is taken to stay superficial initially until it drops away.
- 5 - Angiocath placement and diaphragm retraction (4:44)
	Next, the angiocath for the diaphragmatic retraction suture is placed, followed by those for the pericardial retraction sutures more posteriorly. The diaphragmatic retraction suture is then placed into the central tendon. The first bite is deliberately taken very superficially, just enough to tent it up for the second bite. It is then tied with a knot pusher for haemostasis and retrieved inferiorly through the angiocath. It is important to do this in a slow gentle fashion to minimize the risk of injury to the liver.
- 6 - Port placement & completed setup (5:19)
	The incisions for the robotic ports come next. The right arm is placed posterior to the anterior axillary line, usually in the sixth but occasionally in the seventh ICS. The LA retractor port is inframammary in the fourth ICS in about the midclavicular line. The left arm is anterior to the anterior axillary line, most commonly in the second space in female patients to create more room between it and the working port. Last, the basket vent port is placed just below the main incision.
	We typically use a long port for the right arm and short ports for the LA retractor and left arm. We leave the introducers in for the right and left arm to save a move later, as they usually need to be repositioned slightly after the soft tissue retractor is inserted and it is easy to accidentally remove the port entirely if the introducer has been taken out.
	Tonsillectomy forceps are used to enter the chest and the basket vent is inserted, in a similar fashion to how you insert a chest drain. Of note, the tip is covered by a length of 16Fr urinary catheter to prevent any fat or debris from lodging inside while entering the chest through the fourth ICS just posterior to the working port. This location allows for easier access for haemostasis at the end of the case. The vent is exteriorized through the middle of an XS Alexis soft tissue retractor (Applied Medical, California, USA), which is tightened down. The basket vent is then tucked underneath the edge of the retractor until required after left atriotomy. Finally, the left and right arm ports are manipulated to ensure they are free of the soft tissue retractor and then the introducers are removed.
	The final setup demonstrates the additional working space, which is gained by using the soft tissue retractor, particularly in female patients. However, the result remains a small 4 cm incision, which is usually nicely hidden away and cosmetically pleasing.

Female patients are less frequently referred for multidisciplinary evaluation and intervention for mitral regurgitation \[8\]. Current guideline thresholds, primarily derived from male-predominant cohorts, may be inappropriate for females, possibly contributing to lower concordance with recommended interventions \[9\]. Women referred for surgery are older and with more comorbidities \[7,8\]. They are also more likely to have complex multi-segment prolapse, and mitral annular calcification (MAC), leading to advanced repair strategies, as well as valve replacement, more often being required \[2,8,10,11\].

Optimal exposure is key for complex repairs. Following the steps outlined above ensures consistently good visualization of the valve. Accurate marking of the anterior axillary line, around which the working port is placed, and verifying this line is straight after draping and retraction of the breast, prevents unintended incision displacement. Due to smaller thoracic dimensions and narrower rib spaces, ports may need to be positioned one rib space further apart compared to male patients to prevent instrument conflicts. An important note regarding breast retraction is that this should be released at the end of the case before suturing the wound to achieve an even closure.

We routinely visualize the femoral vessels before chest incision, particularly for those with “borderline” vessel size on CT, due to increased risk of spasm. Our recent series reported a change to the planned approach in 3.4% following identification of small femoral artery diameters after exposure \[4\]. While axillary artery cannulation with a side-graft may serve as an alternative, small femoral arteries usually correlate with similarly small axillary arteries, and this is more useful in the setting of aortoiliac atherosclerosis. Adding a distal perfusion cannula can help in the case of limb ischaemia, but options to address proximal hypoperfusion during the procedure are limited, which underlies our reluctance to utilize very small vessels.

Our deliberately conservative algorithm for selecting patients for robotic mitral repair qualified 60% of those screened, yielding excellent outcomes for both sexes \[4\]. However, females represented just 27% of the robotic group versus 43% undergoing sternotomy. Kandula et al. showed that female patients who present with the same type and degree of mitral disease and similar comorbidities receive the same type of surgical procedures and experience similar outcomes \[12\]. Thus, the sex-based differences in selection for robotic surgery likely reflects differences in referral patterns rather than suitability. Improved education and awareness are essential to address these discrepancies.
