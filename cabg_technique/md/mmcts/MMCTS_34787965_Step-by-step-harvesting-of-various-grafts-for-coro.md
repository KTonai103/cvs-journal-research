## Abstract

One of the key aspects to obtain good long-term outcomes after coronary artery bypass grafting is graft quality. Meticulous graft harvesting is an important technical aspect in successfully performing high-quality coronary surgery and is associated with improved long-term graft patency. Hence, developing surgical skills in this necessary surgical step is of utmost importance in coronary bypass surgery. The following video tutorial presents a step-by-step audiovisual description of the skeletonized harvesting technique of the left internal mammary artery, open and endoscopic radial artery harvesting, and open saphenous vein graft harvesting.

## Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is one of the most commonly performed operations worldwide. Prior to surgical revascularization, successful graft harvesting is required. One of the key aspects to obtaining good long-term outcomes after CABG is graft quality [1, 2], and meticulous graft harvesting is an essential step in ensuring a high quality bypass graft. Hence, acquiring adequate competency in this crucial surgical step of CABG is of utmost importance.


Although graft harvesting is an important aspect of every coronary bypass operation, it is often performed by a less experienced member of the team. Coronary graft harvesting is a fundamental skill that must be mastered by cardiac surgery residents from the early stages of their surgical training, and the importance of harvesting a good quality graft must be emphasized in every cardiac surgical training program. The following video tutorial focuses on the surgical technique of coronary graft harvesting and presents a step-by-step audiovisual description of how to harvest grafts of a skeletonized left internal mammary artery (LIMA), an open and endoscopic radial artery (RA), and an open saphenous vein. Various patients with coronary artery disease undergoing graft harvesting and CABG are presented.

## Patient presentation

For accurate patient/graft selection, some important aspects should be considered:


Previous sternotomy: If the internal mammary arteries were not used in the previous operative procedure, their integrity should be assessed during selective coronarography or, more commonly, by means of computed tomography scans.


Radial artery: The Allen test is a good clinical tool to assess the adequacy of the collateral supply from the ulnar artery (UA). However, Doppler sonography is the standard of care and should be used with all elective patients in whom RA harvesting is planned. The RA graft should be preferably harvested from the non-dominant arm. Although RA harvesting is well tolerated in most patients, it is not ideal in patients who frequently perform specialized manual activities (e.g., painters, pianists, surgeons).


Saphenous vein: Avoid legs with varicose veins. Always ask if the patient has a history of operations for varicose vein removal and deep venous thrombosis. Inspect the legs, looking for signs of chronic venous insufficiency.

## Outcome and discussion

LIMA graft
One remarkable characteristic of the LIMA is its resistance to atherosclerosis and its endogenous production of vasodilative substances such as nitric oxide and prostacyclin [1]. The LIMA also shows a favorable vasodilative response to substances usually used in the postoperative period, such as milrinone and nitroglycerin, and does not vasoconstrict in response to norepinephrine [1]. The endogenous secretion of endothelial substances provides a long-term downstream protective effect against atherosclerosis in the native anastomosed coronary artery. The LIMA also has the ability to remodel and change its diameter to increase the blood flow according to the demand [1].


Pedicled LIMA harvesting has been associated with increased postoperative sternal ischemia and possible wound infection [3]. Skeletonized LIMA reduces sternal ischemia and consequently the risk of postoperative wound infection and sternal dehiscence [3]. Diabetic and obese patients, as well as those undergoing bilateral IMA harvesting, benefit in particular from the skeletonized technique [2]. Although the skeletonized technique might be technically more demanding and time-consuming than a pedicled technique, skeletonized LIMA grafts have higher flow rates and a greater luminal diameter [4] with no observable damage to the endothelium [2] and no negative impact on patency rates in comparison to pedicled LIMA grafts [5].


Radial artery graft
The use of the RA as a coronary bypass graft was initially abandoned for many years, given the poor results due to common postoperative spasm and low early patency rates. The sensitivity of the RA to norepinephrine is comparable to the sensitivity of the LIMA [1]. However, the RA has a greater muscular layer that generates stronger vasoconstriction, accounting for its well-known predisposition to vasospasm. However, the interest in the RA as a bypass conduit revived after long-term angiography studies demonstrated RA grafts to be patent after a follow-up of 15 years, even if the same grafts were initially described as occluded during early angiography [6]. Excessive surgical manipulation of the graft and harvest-associated damage were identified to be responsible for early spasm and occlusion [6]. Moreover, an improved understanding of the graft’s physiology showed that a pedicled, less traumatic harvesting of the RA as well as pharmacologic treatment with calcium channel blockers, papaverine, nitrates, and milrinone significantly reduced and even reverted postoperative vasospasm. These factors led to a resurgence of the RA as an alternative arterial graft.


The RA can also be harvested endoscopically. Advocates of endoscopic harvesting report less postoperative pain and lower wound infection rates after endoscopic RA harvesting in comparison to the open technique.


Saphenous vein graft
The saphenous vein is one of the most commonly used conduits during CABG [1]. Its consistently popular use over time is based on its ease of harvest, availability, and resistance to spasm. However, even if the great saphenous vein has plenty of long-term follow-up studies reporting its usefulness as a coronary graft, a time-related deterioration of the graft has been clearly demonstrated [1]. Therefore, clinical trials have focused on pharmacological strategies (e.g., aspirin, statins) to optimize graft patency.


The dissection site is also a point of controversy. Some surgeons prefer harvesting the vein from the lower leg because of a more consistent vein caliber as well as reduced risk of infection given the greater distance to the perineal area. Nonetheless, other surgeons argue in favor of harvesting from the proximal portion due to superior wound healing ability in the upper leg, especially in patients with chronic peripheral vascular disease. However, due to a greater amount of subcutaneous tissue in the thigh, especially in obese patients, wound closure is more challenging and in the case of a wound infection, its treatment may prove to be particularly problematic. There is, however, no solid evidence supporting one harvesting site over another.


There are 3 different techniques to harvest the saphenous vein: the completely open, the bridged, and the endoscopic harvesting technique. In our center we prefer harvesting the great saphenous vein by means of the completely open technique, because it allows faster harvesting with better graft exposure and less surgical trauma to the conduit. Despite the larger wound surface, we rarely observe wound infections in our center. However, endoscopic harvesting is gaining popularity around the world because of reduced wound-associated complications and improved cosmetic results. Endoscopic saphenous vein harvesting is nonetheless technically more demanding, and the surgical time devoted to harvesting the graft is usually longer in comparison to that of the completely open technique. Moreover, some studies have demonstrated that endoscopic saphenous vein graft harvesting has been associated with higher rates of vein graft failure, myocardial infarction, repeat revascularization, and death [7].


Novel strategies are being developed and adopted to increase long-term saphenous vein graft patency. Pedicled saphenous vein graft harvesting with the "no-touch" technique has been demonstrated to result in improved long-term patency; however, no differences in mortality and in the need for reintervention have been observed after a mean follow-up of 6.8 years [8]. One other novel strategy to improve saphenous vein graft patency has been to provide external support with an external metal stent [9]. Moreover, the long-term benefits of a novel endothelial-damage inhibitor is currently being clinically evaluated in an ongoing multicentric prospective observational registry [10].
